# Making a Home

A comfortable, well-kept home lifts your mood and lets you wake well rested. When you fall asleep under a roof, the room around you is judged. You are told how it rated when you wake, and what it still lacks.

## What makes a room comfortable

A room is the roofed space around you, closed in by walls, doors and windows. Each of these counts once, so a variety of things matters more than a lot of one thing:

| Comfort | What counts |
| --- | --- |
| A covered floor | Wood, stone, marble, tiles, carpet or woven rugs over at least half the floor |
| Finished walls | At least half the walls limewashed or painted (see below) |
| A light | A lamp, lantern, brazier, torch stand or fireplace |
| A bed, a seat and a table | All three; a bedroll is not a bed |
| A work of art | A painting, a tapestry, a woven rug, a glazed or painted pot set down, a statue or rock art, in the room or on its walls |
| A living plant | A filled plant pot or flowers |
| A named house | A custom sign with a name on it, on a wall or inside |
| A place to wash | A wash basin or washstand with water in it, a sink or tap, a shower, a bath or a public bath |
| A clean room | No more than a couple of stains or bits of rubbish, and no pit latrine, outhouse or dung in the room |
| A tidy room | A basket, jar, chest, crate or closet to keep things in, and no more than 5 things left lying on the floor |
| Sound walls | No cracked or badly weathered walls |
| A quiet bedroom upstairs | A bed in a room above the ground floor, with no stove, oven, forge, anvil, loom, dehydrator or workbench in it |

The room rates as **bare**, **cosy**, **comfortable**, **fine** or **splendid** depending on how many it has. Any nine of the twelve make it splendid.

### Wash basins and washstands

Shape a **clay wash basin** from 3 clay, by hand or on the potter's wheel, and fire it like a pot. Hold the fired basin and use it to set it down where you stand. A wooden **washstand** (6 soft wood, Hygiene research) holds a basin and a pitcher. Fill either with a bucket, pipe water to it, or set it beside running water. On **Help intent**, click it to wash your hands, or target another body part to wash that. Each wash takes a little water: a basin holds 30 units and a washstand 50, at 5 a wash.

Stand in a room and use **Judge This Room** (IC tab) to see how it would rate and what is missing. It also tells you how well the room holds its warmth, whether it is draughty, and whether its fire is big enough; see [Keeping a building warm](Guide_to_Construction.md#keeping-a-building-warm).

## What a good home does

- **Mood.** A cosy or better home lifts your resting mood more than a plain roof does. It counts all day after you sleep there, like a good bed.
- **A higher ceiling.** A fine or splendid home lets your mood settle a little higher than living conditions alone otherwise allow.
- **Well rested.** Waking in a comfortable, fine or splendid home leaves you well rested for half an hour, 45 minutes or an hour. You get your breath back faster and grow hungry and thirsty more slowly.

## Using every storey

Each floor of a house is good for something:

- **The cellar.** A room dug out underground stays cool all year, around 12 degrees, whatever the weather. Food keeps longer there, and it is the coolest place to rest in summer. An ice house built there melts more slowly too. See [Food storage](Guide_to_Cooking.md#food-storage).
- **The ground floor.** Keep the kitchen and the workshop here. Floods reach the ground floor and the cellar, and grain, seed and flour left on the floor are spoiled by them. Mice only nest on the ground floor.
- **The rooms upstairs.** Mice never nest upstairs, and floods don't reach it, so it's the safe place for grain and stores. A bed up there, away from stoves and workbenches, makes a quiet bedroom. A chimney built over the hearth below carries its warmth up (see [Keeping a building warm](Guide_to_Construction.md#keeping-a-building-warm)).
- **The loft.** A dehydrator in a roofed room upstairs dries food half again as fast.
- **The roof.** You can see the land below from the roof and shoot down at people standing under the open air. Smoke signals sent from a rooftop fire are seen further away, and from the ground as well.

## Upkeep

Keep the room clean and the walls in repair, and renew the wash on the walls now and then. Rubbish, stains, cracked walls and things left lying about each cost the room a point until you deal with them. Walls weather over the years and can be repaired with their building material (see **[Construction](Guide_to_Construction.md)**).

## Limewash, paint and stain

1. Burn stone or marble in a **lime kiln** to get **quicklime** (see **[Pottery and Lime](Guide_to_Pottery_and_Lime.md)**).
2. Fill a bucket or another open container with at least 20 units of water, then use a lump of quicklime on it. The quicklime slakes the water into 20 units of white **limewash**.
3. To colour it, use a **pigment** on the container (see **[Painting](Guide_to_Painting.md)**). This tints all the limewash in it and uses up one use of the pigment.
4. Use the container on a built wall or a laid floor. Each wall or floor tile takes 5 units.

On walls and stone floors it is a wash or paint. On wooden floors it soaks in as a stain, and plain limewash gives limed, pale wood. Roads, bridges, carpets, thatch and mats cannot be coated.

A coat lasts about two weeks. After that it looks worn and patchy, and the walls no longer count as finished until you give them a fresh coat.

## Dyed furniture and curtains

- When you pad a bed, chair, sofa or stool with **cloth**, you choose a dye for it. Leather padding keeps its own colour.
- To change the colour later, use another sheet of cloth on the padding and pick a new dye.
- Use a sheet of cloth on a cloth curtain to dye it.

The dyes are the same ones you know for tailoring. Weaving gives the natural dyes. Classical Dress and Fine Garments add more, and Textile Mills lets you mix any colour.

## Arranging things

Use **Nudge Object** (IC tab) next to a piece of furniture, a picture, a sign or something lying on the floor to shift it a couple of pixels at a time, up to a quarter of a tile from where it sits. A picture or sign on a wall stays on its wall. **Reset** puts it back on the grid.

## Pottery of your own design

1. Make an **unfired clay vessel** from 3 clay, by hand or on the potter's wheel (Pottery & Cookware).
2. Use it in hand to work it:
   - **Shape**: a cup, bowl, jug, jar, vase, amphora, pilgrim flask or storage jar.
   - **Paint band**: paint a band round the pot with the pigments you carry, as you would a painting (see **[Painting](Guide_to_Painting.md)**). Anything left in the clay colour stays bare.
   - **Glaze**: dip it in a slip or glaze. White and red ochre slip need nothing more. Pottery & Cookware adds black gloss and wood-ash glaze, Glassworking adds turquoise, amber and green lead glazes, and Fine Arts & Leisure adds celadon, tin white and cobalt blue.
3. Fire it in an oven or kiln. It becomes a working container, from a 30 unit cup to a 200 unit storage jar.

A glazed or painted pot set down on a floor or table counts as a work of art.

## Rugs and tapestries

1. Make a **warp** from cloth (Weaving): a small rug (2 cloth), a runner two tiles long (3), a large rug two tiles by two (6), a tapestry (2) or a wide tapestry two tiles across (3).
2. Hold the warp beside a **loom** and use it. Weave the design pixel by pixel with the dyes you know for tailoring, and their blends. Give it a title and finish it.
3. Use a finished rug on a floor tile next to you to lay it. It stretches east, and a large rug north too. Use **Roll Up Rug** on it to take it back.
4. Hang a tapestry on a wall in front of you like a painting. A wide tapestry hangs on a wall to your north, and the wall must run on to your right.

Rugs count as a covered floor where they lie and as art. Tapestries count as art like any picture on the wall.
