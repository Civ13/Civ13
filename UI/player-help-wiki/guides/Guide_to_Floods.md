# Rivers & Floods

On lands with hills, such as [North and South America](../maps/The_Americas.md), [Gaia](../maps/Gaia.md), Europe, Jungle and Asia, rivers rise and fall. A long rain or a spring thaw can push a river over its banks and onto the low ground beside it. This page covers when rivers rise, what floodwater does to you, and how to keep your home out of it.

## When rivers rise

- **Rain.** Storms raise every river whose land they rain on. A storm upstream sends a rise downstream a few minutes later, so a river can climb after the rain has stopped where you are.
- **Snowmelt.** Snow that falls in winter stays on the hills and runs into the rivers in spring. Spring brings the biggest floods.
- **Seasons.** Rivers run high in spring and in the wet season. They run low at the end of summer, in the dry season and under a heat wave.
- **Size.** A big river with a lot of land draining into it rises highest and stays up longest. A small stream rises a little and drops quickly.

Floods come only from rivers, lakes and swamps. The sea does not flood the land.

A flood fills land in order of height. Banks a step above the water go under first. Land behind a ridge stays dry until the water tops the ridge. Water you see standing on flat ground near a river during a storm is a warning that the river is already high.

Rain also gathers in **hollows**: low ground with no way out. A long storm can leave a hollow ankle- or knee-deep even far from any river, and the water soaks away over a few hours. A river that rises over a ridge or a wall fills the land behind it up to the river's own level.

## Walking in floodwater

| Water | What it does to you |
| --- | --- |
| Ankle-deep | A little slower, and your feet get wet. |
| Knee-deep | Slower again. |
| Waist-deep | As slow as a flooded deck. |
| Over your head | You must swim. You tire, slip under holding your breath, and drown just as in deep water. Good swimmers last longer. |

Boots made for wading help in shallow water, as they do in a river's shallows. A boat, a lifejacket or a diving suit keeps you up in deep floodwater. Use **Check Movement** (IC tab) to see how deep the floodwater under you is and how much it slows you.

## Reading the land

After a flood, examine a tree, a wall or the ground. A line of dried silt shows how high the last floods rose there, from ankle-deep up to over your head. The marks fade over a few years, so they show recent floods. Look for them before you choose where to build.

If you examine ground that is under water, you are told how deep the water is.

## Fire

Floodwater puts out fires on the ground, and it puts out campfires that it reaches. A campfire under water can't be lit until the water goes down, so build your fire on high ground.

## Farms and wells

- **Crops drown.** A crop standing in knee-deep floodwater dies after a few minutes, and faster in deeper water. Shallow floodwater only waters it. **Rice** grows in standing water and never drowns.
- **Silt.** A crop that comes through a flood alive leaves fresh silt in its plot, which brings back the soil's goodness. This is why people have always farmed floodplains despite the risk.
- **Wells.** Floodwater that covers a well fouls it, and drinking its water unboiled can give you cholera. When the water has gone down, scrub the well with a bar of soap to clean it. The water in a [dug well](Guide_to_Your_First_Day.md#digging-a-well) rises and falls with the river beside it: wells near a river in flood stand fuller, and at low water a shallow one can go dry.
- **Stores.** Floodwater ruins flour sacks, rots grain and seed, and empties gunpowder pouches left on the floor, and from knee depth it floats small things away toward the river. Keep supplies on a table, which keeps them dry until the water is waist-deep, on high ground, or upstairs.
- **Clothes.** Waist-deep floodwater soaks your clothes, the same as wading in a river. Wring them out or dry them by a fire.

## Sewers

On these lands a flushing toilet needs a drain, or it backs up onto the floor.

- **Drain ditch.** Dig one with a shovel (**Drain Ditch**). Ditches on neighbouring tiles join into one line.
- **Stone drain.** A drain covered with stone slabs, built from 2 stone once your people know **Sanitation**. It joins ditches and other drains the same way.
- **Floor drain.** A stone grate set in a floor, built from 1 stone once your people know **Sanitation**. It joins drains beside it, or a drain on the storey below through a pair of floor sleeves (see [Construction](Guide_to_Construction.md#cables-and-pipes-through-walls-and-floors)). Water spilt on it or right beside it runs away and washes blood, vomit and other slops on that tile down with it, and stains that land on the grate go straight down. Put one in a bathhouse, a butcher's or a sickroom. Foul water from it fouls the outfall like a toilet's waste. It only drains while its line runs somewhere; hair and scum choke it now and then, so examine it and pull it clear by hand.
- **Bath water.** A running shower wets the floor under it about once a minute, and a tub that is turned off slops its water onto the floor beside it. A floor drain on or right beside the shower or tub takes the water away; a closed curtain round a shower keeps it in too. Someone stepping out of a bath or shower drips a little puddle where they step, unless they step onto a **bath mat** (2 cloth, once your people know **Weaving**) laid beside it. Puddles run away down a floor drain like any other water.
- **Where it goes.** Lay the drain from beside the toilet to a river, a lake or the sea, where it spills into the water, or to a **cesspit** (dig one with a shovel). A gap anywhere in the line stops the flow.
- **Cesspits** fill up. Examine one to see how full it is, and dig it out with a shovel when it is full. What comes out is compost for your fields.
- **Draining low ground.** A drain laid in a hollow carries away the rainwater pooled there, as long as it runs to a river, lake or sea. Ditches drain slowly, stone drains faster and **brick sewers** (3 stone bricks, once your people know **Imperial Architecture**) fastest. In a heavy storm a small drain can run full; toilets on it then back up for a while.
- **Flap gates.** When the river floods over the end of a drain, it pushes back up the drain: low ground fills and toilets surge. Hang a **flap gate** (3 wood, **Sanitation**) on the end of the drain where it meets the water. It shuts against the river and opens again when the river falls.
- **Clogs.** Drains silt up now and then; ditches most, brick sewers least. Examine a drain to see if it is choked, and dig it clear with a shovel.
- **Pumping stations.** A flap gate keeps the river out, but while the river is up nothing drains out either. A pumping station built on the end of a drain lifts the water out over the flood, so low ground behind your walls and gates stays dry and toilets keep flushing. Its valve shuts against the river even when it is stopped. Use it with an empty hand to start or stop it.
  - **Steam pumping engine** (12 iron, once your people know **Steam Power**). Feed it coal, charcoal or wood; coal lasts longest. It stops when the fire burns out, so start it when the river rises and stop it when the river falls.
  - **Electric sewage pump** (8 steel, **Utilities**). Lifts more than the steam engine. Lay a powered cable under it or run it from an outlet.
- **Settling tanks** (6 stone bricks, **Germ Theory**). Set one on the end of a drain where it spills into the water, and the waste settles out as sludge instead of fouling the water downstream. Dig the sludge out with a shovel when it fills; it makes compost. Open ditches upstream still foul wells near them.
- **Septic tanks** (6 concrete, **Public Health**). A sealed tank that takes the place of a cesspit. It fouls no wells, holds three times as much, and slowly digests what it holds, so it rarely needs digging out.
- **Keep sewage away from wells.** A well fouls if an open ditch that carries waste runs within two tiles of it, if a cesspit holding waste is within two tiles, or if a drain empties into the water within four tiles. Covered stone drains do not foul anything along their length.
- **Upstream and downstream.** On a river with a current, a drain only fouls wells and aqueduct intakes downstream of where it empties. Take your water above your own outfall.

## Pollution downstream

What goes into a river travels with it. A drain that has carried waste lately, a body or carcass left in the water, and waste dropped in it all foul the stretch of river they lie in, and the water carries that filth down to the next stretch and the one after. Left alone, it clears within a quarter of an hour or so.

- **Scum.** Fouled river water shows a film of scum, thicker the worse it gets. Examine the water up close to be sure.
- **Big rivers dilute.** The same sewage barely touches a wide river but makes a small stream foul.
- **Effects.** Foul water carries more germs for anyone who drinks or washes in it, an aqueduct drawing from it is fouled, and fish grow back more slowly there. A well within three tiles of foul river water draws the filth through the bank and turns dirty. Scrub it with soap once the river runs clean again.
- **What to do.** Put a settling tank on your outfalls, pull bodies out of the river, and if the town upstream is fouling your water, take it from somewhere else.

## Aqueducts

An aqueduct carries river water by gravity to a town below it. Every piece is built from the crafting menu once your people know **Irrigation**. Lay the pieces on touching tiles from the river to where the water is wanted.

- **Intake** (4 stone). Build it on the bank of a river or lake. Sea and coast water won't do, and swamp water is foul.
- **Noria** (12 wood). A water wheel set on a flowing river lifts the water two levels above the river. Still water doesn't turn it.
- **Channel** (2 stone). The ordinary run along the ground. Water can't climb: it only flows into a channel on ground no higher than the water, and it settles to the ground's level as it goes.
- **Arches** (5 stone) and **timber flume** (3 wood). Raised runs that keep the water at its level across a valley or low ground. People can walk underneath.
- **Qanat shaft** (3 stone). An underground run that keeps the water's level through a hill.
- **Clay pipe** (1 clay) and **lead pipe** (1 lead, **Sanitation**). Pipes carry water down a dip and back up to the level it came in at. Lead pipes slowly put lead into the water drawn from a fountain or tap beside them.

Water reaches the pieces about once a minute, and everything beside a wet piece is fed:

- A **public fountain** (6 stone) fills only from the aqueduct. Draw water with a bucket or drink from it.
- A **water tap** (2 bronze, **Sanitation**) fills the same way and holds half as much.
- A **public bath** (4 stone, **Hygiene**) fills and drains about five minutes after the water stops. Undress and bathe in it to get clean and in a better mood. A lit brazier or fire beside it warms the water for a better soak.
- A **cistern** beside a channel fills up.
- A **mill wheel** beside a channel turns as a mill race, even away from a river.
- A clogged **sewer drain** beside a channel is flushed clear.

Keep bodies and waste out of the channels. Anything rotting in a piece fouls all the water below it, and foul water from a fountain or bath can make people ill until the channel is cleaned. Breaking any piece cuts off the water to everything below it, so guard the line in a war.

### Planning waterworks on paper

A ship's draught can hold a plan for one aqueduct or sewer route. Hold a blank draught (see [Guide to Boats](Guide_to_Boats.md#ships-draughts)) with a pen or pencil in your other hand, use it and choose **Draw waterworks on the sheet**.

- Pick **Aqueduct** or **Sewer**, then pick a part and click tiles in order along their edges, starting at the intake (or the first floor drain). The board is 7 tiles square and a route can be up to 24 tiles long. Click a numbered tile with another part selected to swap it, or use **Remove last**.
- An aqueduct starts at an intake beside fresh water and ends at a fountain or cistern. A sewer starts at a floor drain and ends at a stone drain on the water's edge. A sewer tile beside water before the end would spill there too, so the plan flags it.
- Stand on the tile you want as the plan's south-west corner and press **Preview site**. The checks show where water can't climb, where a tile isn't firm dry ground, what is in the way, and warnings such as an uphill sewer or a sewer emptying into fresh water. Only tiles you can see in good light are checked. The list of materials counts only the parts not already built there.
- Press **Save to sheet**. Then use the sheet, stand on the same corner and choose **Peg out the saved route here**. Builder's pegs mark each part still to build, and each one disappears once that part is built. Pegging somewhere else moves the sheet's pegs; **Pull up its pegs** removes them.

The plan doesn't build anything, supply materials or teach research. Build each part from the crafting menu as usual. **Export** and **Import** share the drawing as a code starting `C13WATER`, without its place or pegs.

## Rivers in flood

- **Faster current.** A high river carries swimmers, empty rafts and anything left in the water twice as far. At low water the current is weak.
- **Waterwheels** make up to twice their power when the river is high and as little as half when it is low. Examine a waterwheel to see whether the river is high or low.

## Keeping dry

- **Build high.** The banks just above a river are the first land to flood. A step or two of height keeps a home dry through most floods.
- **Raise the ground.** Put a pile of dirt on a tile and pack it with a stone rammer to raise it one level (see [Tamping paths and grading hills](../maps/The_Americas.md#tamping-paths-and-grading-hills)). Raised ground stays dry until the flood rises above its new height.
- **Build a wall.** A finished dirt, sandbag, rock or sandstone half-wall holds back floodwater one level higher than the ground it stands on, and a concrete half-wall a level and a half. A line of them along a bank keeps the land behind dry until the river rises over the top. A gap anywhere in the line lets the water through. Palisades let water pass between their stakes.
- **Build on stilts or a plinth.** A **stilt platform** (4 wood, once your people know **Primitive Construction**) keeps everything on it dry until the water outside stands two levels deep. A **stone plinth** (4 stone, **Stone Masonry**) does the same for one level. Build your floors, walls and stores on top.
- **Sluice gates.** A **sluice gate** (6 wood, **Irrigation**) set in a gap in a levee holds the river back like the bank around it while it is shut. Draw it up with an empty hand to let floodwater onto the fields behind on purpose: the water leaves fertile silt where it stands. Shut it again to keep the next flood out. A [lever's cord](Guide_to_Levers_and_Mechanisms.md) can draw it up and lower it from a distance.
- **Lifting water out.** A **shaduf** (4 wood, **Basic Agriculture**) or an **Archimedes screw** (8 wood, **Irrigation**) on the edge of a flooded hollow, beside the ground outside it, lifts the pooled water out over the rim while you work it. The screw lifts three times as much as the shaduf. Set a screw beside a treadwheel, horse gin, mill wheel or windmill, or on a line shaft, and it turns on its own and drains the hollow while it is driven.
- **Cisterns.** A **cistern** (10 stone, once your people know **Paved Infrastructure**) stores rainwater for the dry months. Rain falling on it runs in, and so does rain from every roofed tile within two tiles through the gutters, so build it beside your houses. Draw from it with a bucket.
- **River gauge.** Drive a **river gauge** (2 wood, **Basic Agriculture**) in within two tiles of the river and examine it to read how far the river stands below the top of its banks, and how high the last floods rose.
- **Cellars and mines.** When the river rises close to the top of its banks, groundwater seeps into cellars, mines and caves dug beneath nearby ground, up to your knees. If the flood tops the banks, it pours down any open stairs, ladders and holes as well. Bail the water out by using an empty bucket on the wet floor; that keeps the floor around you dry for a while, but the water seeps back while the river stays high. Dig cellars under high ground, away from the river. Floodwater pouring down a tunnel's hole runs along the tunnel and fills it to the roof, a few tiles a minute. Anyone caught in it has one breath to reach shallower water or a ladder before they drown. Bailing can't keep up with that. Fill the hole in or pump the tunnel, and once nothing more pours in, the water slowly soaks away. A tunnel you no longer need can be filled in: four dirt piles close its hole, and a shovel with a dirt pile packs its floor solid so no water stands there (see [Filling tunnels back in](Guide_to_Resource_Extraction.md#filling-tunnels-back-in)).
- **Cellar pumps.** A pump on the cellar floor keeps the floor around it dry while it works:
  - **Lever pump** (6 iron, **Workshops**). Work the handle; the floor within three tiles stays dry for a while after you stop, up to five minutes.
  - **Steam mine pump** (12 iron, **Steam Power**). Feed it coal, charcoal or wood and start it; it keeps everything within seven tiles dry until the fire burns out.
  - **Sump pump** (4 steel, **Utilities**). Lay a powered cable under it or run it from an outlet. It starts by itself when water comes in, and its battery keeps it pumping for an hour if the power fails in a storm.
- **Windmill pump** (12 wood, **Watermills**). Set on the edge of low ground that floods, beside the ground outside it, it lifts the pooled water out whenever the wind blows, with nobody at it. The stronger the wind, the faster it drains. Use it to set or release the brake.
- **Mind your digging.** Lowering ground through a ridge or wall opens a way for the water. The change takes effect within about a minute.
