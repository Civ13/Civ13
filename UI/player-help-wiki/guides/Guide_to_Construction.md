# Guide to Construction

Partial trench digging and filling progress survives world saves, alongside trench layout and contents. Freshwater trenches can also supply crops; see [Farming](Guide_to_Farming.md#watering) for irrigation, connected channels and salinity.

It is truly a marvel, that from this dusty dirt under our feet, these
heaps of rugged stones and those chaotically growing trees could spring
such smooth, even walls in all their rectangular glory. A natural
landscape is a wild beast that needs to be reined in, and reined in it
will be!

---

Construction requires basic knowledge in other areas, such as resource acquirement, which are covered in the **[Guide to Crafting](Guide_to_Crafting.md)**.

There are a few key elements in the area of construction that you could
combine and rearrange as you see fit to raise buildings, lay roads and
develop infrastructure as a whole that is both functional and pleasing
to the eye. These elements are walls, floors, doors, windows, roofs and
barricades along with fences. There are also somewhat distinct
categories of fortifications and lighting.

**To look for specific items, use the “Find in page” command - Ctrl-F.**

## Construction orientation

**Mind that the orientation of some constructions depends on the
direction your character is facing!** For example, some walls are built
vertically if you are facing North or South, or horizontally, if you are
facing East or West. Some wall types also have separate corner elements
for better appearance. All of this is marked in the description of a
specific construction. **N, S, W, E** mean you need to be facing either
North, South, West and East to build a wall the same as the image;
**All** means the construction will look the same regardless of the
direction.

<b>Some wall tiles change their appearance depending on the neighbouring
walls of the same type, so they connect seamlessly to each other.</b>
These walls are marked as **Adaptive**.

## Planning a house on paper

A [ship's draught](Guide_to_Boats.md#ships-draughts) (1 wood, Papermaking) can hold a **house plan**. Drawing one costs nothing and builds nothing: it shows you, before you spend any material, where the game will refuse a roof and what the house will be missing.

Hold a blank ship's draught with a pen, pencil or pigment in your other hand, use the sheet and choose **Draw a house on the sheet**.

<img src="../images/house_plan_designer.png" alt="The house plan window showing the second storey of a timber house: the sheet on the left, the parts to draw with in the middle, and the part description, notes and checks on the right">

- **The sheet** is 20 by 20 tiles with north at the top and shows one storey at a time. The **Storey** buttons go from the ground floor up to the sixth storey. A dot on a button shows that storey has something drawn on it, and the dot turns red when a check found a fault there.
- **Each tile holds three things:** one piece (a wall, door, window, pillar, roof support, stairs or ladder), one floor and one roofing. Pick a part and click or drag to draw it. Right-click clears what the picked tool draws, and the eraser clears the whole tile.
- **A roof is the floor of the storey above**, just as when you build. Roof every tile of a storey, the wall tiles included, before you draw the next storey on top of it.
- **Drag a box** draws the outline of a room when a wall is picked, and fills the box with a floor, a roofing or the eraser.
- **Roof this storey** roofs everything inside the walls with the picked roofing and leaves the tile over stairs and ladders open. **Copy the walls below** stands the walls, windows and pillars of the storey below on the one you are looking at. A door is copied as a window.
- Upstairs, the walls of the storey below show through faintly so you can stand the next walls on them, and the opening over stairs or a ladder is marked.
- **Start from** loads a ready-made cabin, two-storey house or stone tower house to change as you like.
- **Save to the sheet** keeps the drawing. Use the sheet again to see its summary or change it. **Export its design code** (or **Export code** in the window) gives a code you can keep outside the game or give to someone else, and **Import a design code** (or **Import code**) draws a shared house onto any ship's draught. See [Sharing plans as design codes](Guide_to_Boats.md#sharing-plans-as-design-codes).

A wall on the plan stands for every wall of its strength. A timber wall is any wall that carries two storeys, such as a log, plank, bamboo, straw or earth wall. A stone wall is any that carries four, such as stone, brick, clay, sandstone or marble. The **Figures** panel counts the parts and gives rough materials from one usual recipe for each, so treat it as a guide.

**Checks.** The panel on the right judges the drawing as you draw. Click a check to ring its tiles on the sheet; the window steps to the storey it is about.

| Check | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Fix** | The game will refuse this when you build. A roof tile has no support in reach, the walls are too weak for that storey, the ground-floor walls need a stone or concrete floor under them, a wall upstairs has no roof tile under it or nothing strong enough beneath it, or a lone pillar upstairs is asked to hold tiles it is not next to. The rules are the ones under [Roofs](#roofs) and [Upper storeys](#upper-storeys). |
| **Warning** | You can build it, but something is missing: no stairs or ladder to a storey, stairs with a roof straight over them, a room with no door, a tile inside the walls with no roof over it on any storey, or no roof at all. |
| **Note** | Advice: a roofed space that is not closed in by walls, windows and doors, stone walls on soft ground that could never take a third storey, or an upper floor with an open edge to fall from. |

The plan cannot see the ground you will build on, so it treats bare ground as soft. On rock a house can go higher than its plan says.

**Notes of your own.** Pick the **Note** tool, click a tile and type up to 80 characters, for example where the hearth goes or whose room it is. A plan holds 12 notes. They show as a corner mark on the tile, are listed in the Notes panel and travel with the plan and its design code.

### Pegging the plan out

A saved plan can be marked out on the ground, so that you build from pegs and not from memory.

<img src="../images/house_plan_pegs.png" alt="The ground floor of a two-storey house pegged out on grass: string round every tile, with tags for walls, windows, a door, stairs, a roof support and floors">

1. Stand where the **south-west corner** of the drawing should go. North on the sheet is north on the ground.
2. Use the house plan and choose **Peg it out on the ground**. After a few seconds every tile of the ground floor with something drawn on it has a peg, with string round the tile and a tag showing what to build there.
3. Build. A tile's tag shows the piece first, then the floor, then the roof. A peg comes out by itself once everything it asks for stands on its tile. Any wall counts as the wall and any roofing as the roof, so you can build with what you have.
4. For the next storey, lay the roof below it, climb up, stand on that storey and choose **Peg out the storey I stand on**. It lands exactly over the ground floor. Tiles with no floor under them yet get no peg, so peg the storey again as more of the roof below is laid.

- Pegs block nothing. You walk over them, and a click on a pegged tile goes to the tile, so you build on it as usual.
- **Shift-click** a peg to read what is still to build on its tile and any note pinned there. **Alt-click** it to pull up that peg, or every peg of the plan.
- Using the sheet again shows how much the pegs still ask for. Pegging a storey a second time is safe: its old pegs are pulled and only what is not yet built is pegged again, which is also how you bring the pegs up to date after changing the drawing.
- A tile with a note keeps its peg after it is built, until someone pulls it.
- **Pull up the pegs and forget the site** removes every peg and lets you peg the same plan out somewhere else.
- A plan that would run off the edge of the land is not pegged. Pegs on rock or water are counted in the message so you know to clear or move.

## Construction materials

A few more details about particular materials.

### Clay

**[Clay](../Full_Crafting_List.md#clay)** is obtained from **[dirt](Guide_to_Crafting.md#soil)**:

- Dig any dirt tiles with a shovel to get dirt piles. Underground, a shovel also digs dirt out of soft cave walls, rubble and tunnel shafts (see [Resource Extraction](Guide_to_Resource_Extraction.md#digging-for-dirt-underground)).

- Fill a container (a bucket, a barrel etc.) with water, then click dirt piles with it.

- 10 units of water + 1 dirt pile = 1 clay

### Concrete

Concrete floors, roofs, pillars and bridge spans are built from **concrete blocks**, and concrete blocks come out of a **cement mixer**.

| | Station | Cost | You need to know |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <img src="../images/Cement_mixer.png"> | **Cement mixer** | 8 iron | Reinforced Concrete |

1. Use **quicklime**, **sand** and **stone blocks** on the mixer to load its drum. It holds 20 of each. Quicklime comes from a [lime kiln](Guide_to_Pottery_and_Lime.md#lime-kiln).
2. Crank the mixer with an **empty hand**. Every 4 seconds it uses 1 quicklime, 2 sand and 2 stone and tips out <img src="../images/Concrete_blocks.png"> **4 concrete blocks** beside it. It keeps turning until the drum runs short or you step away.
3. Use the concrete blocks in your hand like any other building material: a concrete floor or roof builder takes 1 block, a pillar 2 and a bridge span 6.

Fasten or unfasten the mixer with a wrench, and hit it with a hammer on **Harm intent** to take it apart; whatever is in the drum drops out. Like other machines it is too heavy for a ladder, so [hoist it](#hoisting-heavy-loads) if it has to change storeys.

### Bricks

Most bricks and blocks are made with **[Clay](../Full_Crafting_List.md#clay)**, except for stone bricks:

  - First, make batches of unfired bricks.

  - Put the unfired bricks in either **[a campfire, an oven or a stove](Guide_to_Crafting.md#campfire-iron-oven-and-wood-stove)**.

  - Cook until done.

Bricks, cement bricks and clay blocks pile up like roofing, fired or unfired: up to 25 in one pile, and the pile's name shows how many it holds. Drop a brick on a pile of the same bricks and it joins the pile. Use the pile in your hand on more of the same bricks to pick up all of them from that tile. To take a single brick off a pile you are holding, click the pile with your other, empty hand. Laying a foundation or adding to a wall uses one brick from the pile. A whole pile of unfired bricks goes in the fire at once and comes out as one pile (each clay block still makes two), and the brick mould bench turns out each batch as one pile.

**Hover the mouse cursor over the icon to see the material cost.**

## Walls

A tile holds one wall, door or window. A wall can stand on a floor, and a door can hang in a doorway or archway.

<b>Deconstruction</b>

Any kind of wood walls could be set on fire by hitting them with a
torch. A wall will burn down in a few minutes, destroying the roof tiles
supported by the wall as well (more in the
**[Roofs](#roofs)** section). **Remove the grass and the wood floor around the wall if you are going to burn it!** Otherwise the fire
will very likely spread from the burning wall.

Another way is simple brute force - hit a wall with melee weapons, and
it will go down sooner or later. Stone walls, the strongest ones, take
around 100 hits to bring down, or fewer if your strength is high and you
apply a weapon with high damage.

<u><b>Log</b></u>

The strongest timber wall, raised in steps. 4 wood logs per tile, then an axe and a little clay, hay or straw. Corner is the
same for all directions.

1. Choose **log wall** or **log wall corner** in the wood crafting menu. This lays the **sill**, the bottom log (1 log). It is low, so shots often fly over it.
2. Use a log on the sill to add the first courses. The wall is now waist high.
3. Use another log to add the upper courses. The wall is head high and blocks sight.
4. Use a fourth log to lay the **top plate**. The wall is now full height.
5. Use an **axe or hatchet** on it to notch and fit the corners. This makes it as strong as it gets.
6. **Chink** the gaps between the logs with 1 clay, or 2 hay or straw. Until then the wind blows through: a room walled with unchinked logs loses much more warmth.

Notching and chinking can be done in either order. A hammer takes a half-built wall down and gives back the logs laid so far. A 6x7 cabin, about 20 by 24 feet, takes about 88 logs with its roof supports.

## Roofs

Roofs provide shelter from bad weather, e.g. rains, blizzards and
sandstorms, as well as a nice cool shade.

Roofs are supported by walls, windows, doors, wooden roof supports and stone pillars and
columns. ***Wooden mine supports do not hold the buildable roof!***

How far each support reaches, counted in tiles from the support to the roof tile (diagonals count as one):

- **A wall or a window** (a window frame or glazed window) holds the roof on its own tile and every tile touching it.
- **A door** holds only the roof on its own tile.
- **A stone, marble, sandstone or concrete pillar** holds the roof up to 2 tiles away.
- **A wooden or bamboo roof support** holds the roof up to 3 tiles away.

Fences, palisades, sandbags, snow walls, empty wall frames and walls that are still being built do not hold a roof. In a room more than two tiles across, the middle of the room is too far from the walls: put a roof support or pillar in the middle first. If the game refuses a roof, it tells you how far away the nearest support is and how far that support reaches.

Roofing piles up, up to 25 pieces in one pile, and the pile's name shows how many it holds. Drop roofing on a pile of the same roofing and it joins the pile. Use the roofing in your hand on more of the same roofing to pick up all of it from that tile. To take a single piece off a pile you are holding, click the pile with your other, empty hand. Each roof tile you build uses one piece. Roof tiles fired in a kiln come out in one pile.

### Weathering and repairs

Walls and roofs made of fibre (straw, thatch and the like), earth and timber slowly wear out with the weather. Fibre lasts 16 game years, earth 32 and timber 64. Examine a wall or roof to see how many seasons it has left; in its last year it warns you and names the item that repairs it, and people nearby get a warning when a piece enters its last year and again in its last season.

Building well makes a house last. A wall with a roof over it or beside it weathers a quarter slower, and so does a wall standing on rock or on a stone, concrete or steel floor. A wall with both lasts twice as long.

A worn building gives fair warning before anything falls:

- **Cracks** appear on a wall at half its life, deepen at a quarter and are at their worst in its last year. In that last year it also creaks and sheds a little dust each season.
- **A worn-out wall becomes a ruin** instead of vanishing. A ruin still stands, but it holds up no roof and no upper storey. Repair it with its building material, at the full cost of the wall, and it is a wall again. Left alone, it crumbles away after another year.
- **A roof that loses a worn-out support sags first.** Everyone who can see it is warned, and it falls about a minute later unless you put a wall or support under it in time. A roof whose support is knocked down, burnt or dismantled still falls within seconds.
- A worn-out roof falls apart whether or not it is supported.

- **Roofs** are repaired with matching roofing: use it on the roof, or use it while facing the roof. One piece of roofing is used up.
- **Walls** are repaired with their building material. The repair costs the missing share of what the wall took to build, at least one unit. A pile of a wall's own bricks or clay blocks repairs it too and gives up only the bricks the repair needs.

**To construct a roof, take a respective roof builder and either click
it in your hand, or press Ctrl-Z or Z in the hotkeys mode.** A roof tile
will be constructed in the tile in front of you, the one you are facing,
if there is a suitable support nearby.

**To deconstruct a roof, click it with the hammer.** Either stand
outside the roof, so you can see it, or hold Alt and click the tile with
the roof. An additional "Turf" tab will appear in the upper right corner - click the roof there with the hammer in the active hand.

**If roof supports are removed with the roof still present, the roof
will collapse! Collapsing roof may severely injure and even kill those
in the roofed area, or even standing next to it.**

**To take down a roof support, pillar or column, click it with a hammer** (not on harm intent, which strikes it instead). A wooden or bamboo support takes a few seconds, a stone one longer, and you get back the 2 wood, bamboo, stone, marble, sandstone or obsidian it cost; a concrete pillar gives nothing back. The game will not let you take down a support while a roof near it, or a storey above it, stands on it alone: put up another support or a wall nearby first, or take the roof down.

**To take down a wall, click it with a hammer** the same way. A wooden wall takes a few seconds, a stone, brick, clay or concrete wall longer and a metal wall longest; you get back what a broken wall would give, never more than it cost. A wall that a roof, or a storey above, still rests on will not come down: the game tells you how much roof would fall, so put up a pillar or another wall nearby first. Walls that hold nothing up, such as garden walls or an inner wall under a roof that other walls already carry, come down freely.

### Upper storeys

A roof is also the floor of the storey above it. Each roof tile you build adds a walkable **upper floor** on the level above, and the game says "finishes building the roof and upper floor" when it does. Before you build, the roof builder tells you whether a floor will be added and, if not, why. To finish an upper floor, stand on it and build a wood, stone or other floor there: it replaces the plain upper floor and the roof below stays. The game refuses a roof that the structure below cannot carry and tells you why, before any material is used. Roofs that already stand are not affected by these rules.

- **Extending an upper floor:** craft a **floor cover** (2 wood), stand at the edge of an upper floor and click the open air beside you with it, or face that spot and use the cover in hand. The new floor gets a roof under it, so the walls and pillars below must be able to hold that roof, and the game tells you if they can't. A roof that has no floor above it yet takes the new floor as its own.
- **Taking up a floor upstairs:** a hammer takes a finished floor, such as wood or stone, back up to the plain upper floor, and the ceiling below stays. Hammering the plain upper floor itself, or the roof under it from below, removes both, and anything on that tile falls. The game asks before it does that.
- **Fire upstairs:** a burning finished floor burns down to the plain upper floor, not through the ceiling. The upper floor of a stone or concrete roof does not burn.
- **Living upstairs:** beds on an upper floor work for Sleep like beds on the ground, and hay bales can be stacked in an upstairs loft.

How many storeys a land allows:

| Land | Storeys |
| --- | --- |
| Every land, including [Gaia](../maps/Gaia.md) and [North and South America](../maps/The_Americas.md) | up to ten levels above the ground, as high as the supports carry (see below) |

#### Building a second storey, step by step

1. **Plan the foundation now** if you might ever want a third storey: lay a stone, brick or concrete floor wherever a ground-floor wall or pillar will stand (see Foundations below). You cannot add it under a wall later without taking the wall down.
2. Build the ground-floor walls or pillars. Walls hold the roof tiles right next to them; a stone pillar reaches 2 tiles and a wooden roof support 3.
3. Roof the whole footprint, **including the tiles the walls stand on**: stand inside, face each tile and use the roof builder. A tile with no roof has no floor above it, so roofing only the inside leaves you nowhere to put the upper walls.
4. Get onto the upper floor (see [Getting between storeys](#getting-between-storeys)).
5. Build the upper walls or pillars **on that floor, on top of or right next to** the walls below. A wall standing on open air holds nothing.
6. Roof the upper storey the same way. Every tile needs a wall or pillar right next to it, or supports on two sides.

How high a building may go depends on its weakest support:

| Supports | Carry up to |
| --- | --- |
| Wood, bamboo, straw, earth and tent walls, wooden and Nordic roof supports, doors | 2 storeys |
| Stone, brick, clay, sandstone and marble walls, stone pillars, columns | 4 storeys |
| Concrete walls (cement bricks) and concrete pillars | 6 storeys |

- **Foundations:** a third storey or higher needs ground-floor supports that stand on rock or on a stone, brick, marble, sandstone, slate, concrete or steel floor. Lay that floor first, then build the wall or pillar on it. Supports on soil, sand, grass or a wood floor carry two storeys at most, whatever they are made of: a fortress brick wall built straight on grass still stops at two.
- **Supports above supports:** a wall or pillar on an upper floor only counts when a support strong enough for that storey stands on the floor below it, on the same tile or right next to it, all the way down to the ground. A stone wall above a wooden one is only as strong as the wood.
- **Spans:** from the second storey up, a single distant pillar is not enough. Each new roof tile needs a wall or pillar right next to it, or supports within reach on two different sides. Ground-floor roofs keep the plain rule shown in the image above.

A sound four-storey house therefore starts with a stone floor, has stone walls or pillars on every storey standing over the ones below, and has no room wider than its pillars can hold from two sides.

A concrete tower goes up to six storeys the same way: lay a concrete or stone floor under every ground-floor wall and pillar, build concrete walls from cement bricks or concrete pillars on every storey, each one over the support below it, and roof every storey, wall tiles included. Every storey must be concrete all the way down: a concrete wall standing on a stone storey is only as strong as the stone. The roof of the sixth storey leaves a walkable roof terrace on top.

**Adding a foundation to a standing house:** put a wooden roof support inside first so nothing falls, then take down one ground-floor wall at a time with a hammer, lay a stone or brick floor on that tile and rebuild the wall on it. Taking away a ground-floor wall, pillar or stone floor makes every storey above it check its support again, and any storey that the rest of the house can't carry comes down.

#### Getting between storeys

- **Climb up:** face a wall with a free hand and use **IC > Climb Up**, or click the wall with grab intent. You climb onto the floor above it.
- **Climb down:** stand at the edge of an upper floor with a free hand and click the open air next to you with grab intent, or use **IC > Climb Down**. After a few seconds you are on the ground below that spot. It only works for a single storey onto clear ground or shallow water. Walking off the edge instead is a hard fall.
- **Wooden ladder:** craft a wooden ladder (4 wood) and use it on the open air next to an upper floor, or on the ground under open space. It stands between the two levels: click either end to climb, and walking onto the top end doesn't make you fall. Click an end with grab intent and a free hand to take the ladder down again.
- **Stairs:** see Stairs under [Floors](#floors) below. Leave the space above them open. You can build them from below, or from the upper floor by facing an opening in it.
- **Ramps:** a longer, gentler way up that takes carts and wheelchairs. See Ramps under [Floors](#floors) below.
- **Trapdoors and hatches:** a wooden trapdoor (4 wood, Primitive Construction) or an iron hatch (6 iron, Iron Smithing) closes an opening between levels: the open air over stairs or a ladder, a hole in an upper floor, or a dug hole. Stand beside the opening and use it there, then choose whether its inside is above or below. Shut, it is floor: you can walk across it, nothing falls through, and nobody climbs the stairs or ladder through it. Open, anything lying on it drops through. It won't open while someone stands on it. It takes the same locks, latches, bar brackets, bars and slide bolts as a door (see [Doors](#doors)), with above and below as its two sides. From below, work it from the ladder or stairs under it: click them to push it open, and use keys, locks, bars and tools on them to reach it. It comes out again with a hammer (wooden) or wrench (iron) from its inside.
- **Looking down:** from an upper floor or a roof you can see the level below through open air, with whoever and whatever is there. It is drawn darker than your own level, and darker again for each level further down, so you can tell what is beside you from what is below you. Clicking open air still means the open air, but a gun or bow fired at the open air over someone below drops onto them, and aiming down is steadier than shooting across level ground. Nobody below can shoot up through a floor.
- **Looking up:** with open air straight above you (open sky over a built-up area, a stairwell, a hole in a roof or the foot of a ladder), use **IC > Look Up** or **Alt+click** the up arrow to see the level above: who stands on the roofs and walls around you. A roof or a floor over your head blocks the view. Move, or click the label at the top of the screen, to look back down.
- **Up and down buttons:** the HUD has an up arrow and a down arrow. They use whichever of the ways above is at hand: a ladder on or beside you, stairs under you, a wall beside you with a floor on top, or an open edge beside you. The down arrow also gets you out of a tree or off the top of a vehicle. Swimming in the open sea, the down arrow dives to the seabed and the up arrow brings you back to the surface. Hover over a button to see what it will do. A button is greyed out when there is no way up or down from where you stand. You can also give both a key under **OOC > Keybindings**.

#### Balconies and railings

A balcony is an upper floor that reaches out past its walls: put a [corbel](#pillars-buttresses-and-corbels) under the wall's edge and the wall holds a floor one tile further out. Walking off its edge is a hard fall, so put a railing along it.

**To build a railing, stand on the floor next to the edge and face the drop**, then pick it from the material's crafting menu under **railings**. It runs along that side of the tile you stand on. Each side of a tile takes one railing. Build two on a corner tile, facing each way, to turn the corner.

| Railing | Made from | Research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wooden railing** | 2 finished planks | Primitive Construction |
| **Stone balustrade** | 3 stone | Stone Masonry |
| **Sandstone balustrade** | 3 sandstone | Stone Masonry |
| **Brick railing** | fired bricks: use them in hand on a floor and pick **Brick Railing** | Brickmaking |
| **Wrought iron railing** | 2 iron | Iron Smithing |
| **Steel pipe railing** | 2 steel | Steelmaking |
| **Glass balustrade** | 3 glass | Modern Construction |

Every kind also comes as a **gate**, which costs one more of the same material (fired bricks: **Brick Railing Gate**). Click a gate to open or shut it. Use a gate where a ladder or the top of a stair meets the balcony.

- **Nobody walks past a railing.** You can still climb over it with grab intent. If the other side is a drop, the game asks first, because you will fall a storey.
- **Climbing down** over the open edge past a railing takes a little longer.
- **Throwing someone over:** a strong grab can throw someone over a railing, and over a balcony that means a fall.
- **Shots and thrown things fly over** a railing. If you lie down behind a stone, sandstone or brick balustrade, it stops some of the shots coming at you from the far side.
- **A hammer takes a railing down** and gives back what it cost. A railing falls with the floor it stands on, and a hard enough blow breaks it.
- Railings work on any floor, not just upstairs: along a roof terrace, a wall walk or the edge of a stairwell.

#### Hoisting heavy loads

What you pull comes with you between storeys when it makes sense:

- **Stairs and earth ramps:** people, animals, crates and loose items come up or down with you.
- **Ladders:** you need both hands for the rungs, so only people, small animals and loose items come along. Crates, furniture and big animals such as horses go by the stairs.
- **Timber, stone and concrete ramps:** everything stairs take, plus hand carts, shopping carts and wheelchairs. Someone sitting in a wheelchair can roll up them on their own.
- **Stone and concrete ramps with a helper:** the heavy things below can be hauled up too, if a second person stands beside you or the load. It takes as long as a heavy hoist.
- **Otherwise:** anvils, furnaces, kilns, ovens, mills, saw mills, workshop machines, engines, cannon, printing presses and vehicle parts are far too heavy, and wheeled carts and wheelchairs can't be dragged up steps. They need **shear legs**, a lift or a ramp.

| | Station | Cost | You need to know |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <img src="../images/Shear_legs.png"> | **Shear legs** | 10 wood | Primitive Construction |

Shear legs move one unfastened object at a time between a storey and the one above or below it. The load travels through open air, so stand the legs beside the drop: on the ground against the building, or on the upper floor at its edge.

- **Raise or lower:** pull the load next to the legs, then drag it onto them and choose **Raise it to the level above** or **Lower it to the level below**. To raise it, there must be open air over the load and a clear floor tile within 2 tiles of the legs upstairs. To lower it, there must be open air beside the legs with clear ground under it.
- **Haul up from below:** click the legs with an **empty hand** and pick something lying below them. It lands on the nearest clear tile beside the legs.

A haul takes 6 seconds, or 10 for one of the heavy objects above. Stay beside the legs until it finishes. Unfasten a machine with a wrench before you sling it. Light objects such as crates can be hoisted too.

Fasten or unfasten the legs with a wrench, and hit them with a hammer on **Harm intent** to take them apart. The legs themselves can be dragged up stairs.

A **treadwheel crane** hoists the same way but reaches **3 tiles**. It takes 16 wood plus a shaft, two bearings and a drive belt, and you need to know Imperial Architecture.

<img src="../images/Treadwheel_crane.png">

A treadwheel, horse gin, mill wheel or windmill turning next to the legs or the crane speeds every haul. See [Mechanical power](Guide_to_Pottery_and_Lime.md#mechanical-power).

Two later cranes carry their own engine. They only haul while they have fuel, each haul burns a little, and a haul takes about a third of the usual time.

| | Crane | Made from | Research | Reach | Fuel |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <img src="../images/Steam_crane.png"> | **Steam crane** | 20 iron, 1 gear set, 1 shaft, 2 bearings | Steam Power | 4 tiles, one level up or down | coal, charcoal, wood, planks or bamboo used on the crane. One coal pays for 4 hauls, one charcoal for 3, one piece of wood for 1. It holds 40 hauls. |
| <img src="../images/Tower_crane.png"> | **Tower crane** | 30 steel, 2 gear sets, 1 shaft, 2 bearings | Modern Construction | 6 tiles, up to **3 levels** up or down | diesel, biodiesel or gasoline poured from a container used on the crane. A haul burns 5 units. It holds 60 hauls. |

With the tower crane, drag the load onto it and pick how many levels to raise or lower it. The load only travels through open air: every level it passes on the way up must be open above the load, and on the way down it follows a gap in the floors within reach of the crane. Examine either crane to see how many hauls of fuel it has left.

#### Lifts

A lift carries you, and whatever stands on the platform with you, up and down a shaft between storeys.

| | Part | Made from | You need to know |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <img src="../images/Wooden_lift.png"> | **Wooden lift** | 12 wood | Imperial Architecture |
| <img src="../images/Lift_bell.png"> | **Lift bell** | 2 wood | Imperial Architecture |
| <img src="../images/Lift_car.png"> | **Lift car** | 12 iron | Steam Power |
| <img src="../images/Steam_lift_engine.png"> | **Steam lift engine** | 15 iron | Steam Power |
| <img src="../images/Electric_lift_motor.png"> | **Electric lift motor** | 10 steel | Electricity |
| <img src="../images/Lift_call_button.png"> | **Lift call button** | 2 copper | Electricity |

- **The shaft:** build the platform on the bottom storey and leave the same tile open on every storey above it. The platform stops at each storey the open column reaches. Laying a floor across the shaft cuts it off there. While the platform is at a stop, it counts as floor and you can walk over it.
- **Riding:** stand on the platform and click it, then pick a stop. Everything loose on the platform rides with you. A wooden lift won't take anvils, furnaces, machines and the other loads that need shear legs; an iron **lift car** takes them.
- **Winding:** on its own, a lift is wound by hand by whoever rides it, which takes 6 seconds a storey. A treadwheel, horse gin, mill wheel or windmill turning beside any stop of the shaft winds it faster.
- **Steam lift engine:** set it beside any stop of the shaft, bolt it down with a wrench and load it with coal, charcoal or wood like a steam crane. Each storey travelled burns one haul of fuel, and it holds 40. The lift moves about three times faster than by hand.
- **Electric lift motor:** set it beside any stop, bolt it down with a wrench and connect a powered cable to it. It draws power only while the lift moves, about a second a storey.
- **Calling the lift:** put a **lift bell** or a **lift call button** beside the shaft on any storey and use it to bring the lift there. With an engine or a motor the lift comes on its own. A hand-wound lift can't: the bell rings, and whoever is on the platform has to wind it down to you.

Examine a lift to see how many stops its shaft has and what drives it, and a steam engine to see how much fuel it has left.

#### Earthworks: steam roller and steam shovel

Two steam machines do the heavy groundwork. Pull each into place, then use coal, charcoal, wood, planks or bamboo on it to stoke it: one coal pays for 4 jobs, one charcoal for 3 and one piece of wood for 1, up to 40 jobs. Examine a machine to see how much fuel it has.

| | Machine | Made from | Research |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <img src="../images/Steam_roller.png"> | **Steam roller** | 20 iron, 1 gear set, 2 shafts, 2 bearings | Rail & Steam |
| <img src="../images/Steam_shovel.png"> | **Steam shovel** | 20 iron, 1 gear set, 1 shaft, 2 bearings | Steam Power |

**Steam roller.** It lays a straight road in one go.

1. Leave it unfastened, and use **stone** on it to fill its hopper (up to 60).
2. Stand straight behind it, so the road will run away from you, and click it with an **empty hand**.
3. Pick the road: roman road, cobble street or modern road, from the ones you know how to make.
4. Stay beside where it started until it finishes. It paves the tile it stands on and up to 11 more, one stone a tile, and ends on the last tile it laid.

It stops early at water, a wall, anything in the way or a tile that already has a floor. If you walk off before it finishes, no road is laid.

**Steam shovel.** Fasten it with a wrench, then click it with an **empty hand**. Choose a job:

- **Dig loose earth:** it digs the loose dirt and sand out of the bare ground within 2 tiles and heaps it at its foot: up to 12 dirt piles or sand in a job. It does not dig under floors.
- **Cut the ground down a level:** it lowers up to 12 tiles of bare ground within reach by one level, never the tile it stands on, and heaps the earth at its foot as dirt piles. The usual limits on grading apply (see [Tamping paths and grading hills](../maps/The_Americas.md#tamping-paths-and-grading-hills)).

## Damage and collapse

Walls, pillars, doors and roofs can be broken by more than tools and fire.

- **Crashes and rams.** A car, truck, bus, tank or train that drives into a wall, pillar, door or barricade hits it with a force that depends on its weight and speed. A part that gives way lets the vehicle through, one gear slower; a part that holds stops it dead. Either way the vehicle's front takes damage and the people inside are thrown about. A plane flying low into a building does the same, then breaks up where it hit, and a fuelled plane sets wood and thatch alight.
- **Materials.** Thatch, straw and cloth give way first, then wood, then clay and earth, then stone and brick, then concrete; metal walls are the toughest. A cracked wall shows its damage and can be repaired like any other.
- **Map buildings.** The walls of the map's own buildings can be broken this way too.
- **Collapse.** When a wall or pillar goes, every roof that relied on it creaks and sheds dust for a few seconds, then falls if nothing else holds it. A falling roof hurts anyone under it and lands on the floor below; a heavy roof can break a weak floor and bring that storey down too, with the walls and pillars standing on it. A worn-out support still sags for about a minute first, so there is time to prop it.
- **Rubble.** A collapse leaves rubble you can walk over. Clear it by hand or with a shovel to get back part of what it was built from.

### Pillars, buttresses and corbels

Roof supports and stone or concrete pillars are solid: people, animals and vehicles cannot pass through them, so leave room to walk round them. Shots sometimes hit a pillar instead of passing it.

These are built against a wall of the building, facing it. Build them from the material's crafting menu while facing the wall; they turn to the wall they serve. Examine one to see which wall it braces.

| Part | Made from | Research | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Buttress** | 6 stone | Stone Masonry | Built right against a stone, brick or concrete wall. The wall carries one storey more than its material would, and stands twice as well against a blow that pushes it towards the buttress. |
| **Flying buttress** | 10 stone | Imperial Architecture | A pier two tiles out from a stone, brick or concrete wall, with an arch to it. It does what a buttress does, and the wall holds an upper floor one tile further out, for a wider hall without inner pillars. |
| **Stone corbel** | 1 stone | Stone Masonry | A bracket under the edge of any built wall. The wall holds an upper floor one tile further out: a balcony or an overhanging storey. |
| **Wooden corbel** | 1 wood | Primitive Construction | The same in timber. It burns. |

A hammer (not on harm intent) takes a buttress or corbel down, unless a roof stands only because it braces its wall. If a buttress or corbel is broken, the roofs it helped hold check whether they still stand.

## Cables and pipes through walls and floors

A built wall can carry one power cable and one pipe inside it.

1. **Cut a chase.** Use a chisel or a pickaxe on the wall, not on Harm intent. Wood and clay walls take a chisel; stone, brick, concrete and metal walls need a pickaxe and take longer. The chase costs the wall a tenth of its strength.
2. **Run a cable.** Stand straight in front of the chased wall and use a cable coil on it. One cable runs through the wall in the direction you face and joins the cables on either side.
3. **Fit a pipe.** Build the clay or lead water pipe, factory pipe or sewer pipe on your own tile beside the wall, then use a wrench on the wall, not on Harm intent. The pipe slides into the wall and joins its neighbours as before.

Examine the wall to see what runs through it. When the wall breaks, the cable snaps and the pipe bursts.

**Through floors.** A **floor sleeve** (1 clay, Irrigation) lets pipes cross between storeys. Set one sleeve on a tile and another at the same spot on the level above or below, and the pipes on the two tiles join. Factory pipes join both ways, since a pump drives them. Aqueduct water and drains only run downhill: a water pipe or drain over a sleeve feeds the one under the sleeve below, never the other way. To bring water up a storey, pump it through factory pipes or fill a cistern upstairs. A **floor drain** lets spilt water and slops run down to the drains; see [Sewers](Guide_to_Floods.md#sewers). Power already crosses between storeys through cable risers; see the [Guide to Power](Guide_to_Power.md).

## Keeping a building warm

A roof keeps off the rain, but on its own it only breaks the wind, a few degrees warmer than outside. Warmth comes from a closed room: the roofed tiles inside its walls, doors and windows. Use **Judge This Room** (IC tab) to see how well a room holds its warmth.

**What the room is built from.** Each wall, door and window round the room counts:

| Edge | Holds warmth |
| --- | --- |
| Caves and the map's own walls | Very well |
| Earth, clay, adobe, sandbag and snow walls | Very well |
| Stone, brick, thatch and hide walls | Well |
| Wood and concrete walls; a closed door | Fairly well |
| Bamboo and cloth walls; glass windows; metal walls | Poorly |

**Draughts.** An open door, an empty window frame, a missing wall or a roofed floor that runs straight out under the sky is a gap. A room with a gap is draughty and keeps only half its warmth. Close the door behind you.

**Making it warmer.**

- **Line the walls.** Use 2 straw, cloth or wool on a built wall, not on Harm intent, to line it. Wool keeps in the most. A wall takes one lining, and examining it shows what it is lined with.
- **Curtains.** A closed curtain over a window keeps in more than glass alone, and one hung in a doorway or an empty frame stops most of the draught. An open curtain does not.
- **Tapestries and floors.** A tapestry on a wall, and rugs, boards or carpet over most of the floor, help a little.

**Fires.** A lit brazier, heat source, fireplace, big oven or running engine heats the room it stands in. A small, well-built room is warmed by one hearth; a large or draughty hall needs several fires, and a brazier in a barn with open doors does little. Out in the open, a fire only warms people within two tiles of it.

**Chimneys.** A **chimney** (6 stone, Primitive Construction) carries a fire's warmth up to the storeys above. Build it on the upper floor straight over a fireplace, oven or brazier. It warms the room it stands in with half the heat of the fire below, and a second chimney on the floor above that carries half again. The top of the stack smokes while the fire below burns. Examine a chimney to see whether it is drawing heat.

Thick walls work the other way in hot lands: a closed earth or stone room stays cooler than the open in a desert summer.

## Floors

No more sloshing or plodding through the mud and snow! Walk the
civilized way, swiftly and with resonant "top-top" footfalls! Not only
that - floors on the surface prevent digging upwards from the
underground. **Floors can be removed with the hammer.**

A tile holds one floor. To lay a different floor, remove the old one with the hammer first. A floor cover or the upper floor of a storey is the exception: stand on it and build any other floor, such as a wood floor, and the new floor replaces it. The floor cover is used up, and an upper floor keeps the roof below it. Upstairs, the hammer takes a finished floor back to the plain upper floor instead of removing it (see [Upper storeys](#upper-storeys)).

<u><b>Stairs</b></u>

Stairs lead up to the storey above when the space right above them is left
open (no floor or roof over them) and there is a floor at the top to step onto.

them, to climb up; you come out on the floor just past the stairwell. To come

the top step and rails, so you can tell it from a drop. Stairs
under a roof or ceiling are only decoration.

<img src="../images/stairwell_top.png" alt="The open air over stairs, seen from the floor above, shows the top step, rails and arrows pointing down">

hammer to make an opening (a finished floor first comes back to the plain upper
floor; hammer that too and confirm), stand beside it on the floor and face it, then build
the stairs. They go on the ground under the opening and lead back up to the tile
you stand on. The ground under the opening must be clear: take up any floor
there with a hammer first. It only works one storey down, onto ground or onto a
floor that is held up from below. You can also climb down first and build the
stairs from below, facing the side you want to come out on.

## Doors

**Hover the mouse cursor over the images to see the cost, the material and
the direction (how it looks when built while facing either N, S, W or E.
"All" means a door always looks the same.)**

When you finish building a door, you are asked which side is the **inside**:
the side in front of you, the side behind you, or neither (for an inner door
or a gate). An arrow briefly marks the inside tile, and examining the door
later tells you which way its inside faces and which side you are on. The
hinges and fittings go on the inside. Any door can take a fitted lock (see
**Locks** below). A jail door built without a key takes any fitted lock, and
one built tied to a key, as described below, can still take a padlock beside
its own lock.

Doors are built in one step, then finished by fitting parts to them: a
closer, spring hinge or doorstop ([Door fittings](#door-fittings)), a lock
([Locks](#locks)), bar brackets or a slide bolt ([Bars, bolts and
bracing](#bars-bolts-and-bracing)), and a peep-hole, hatch, mail slot or pet
door. Fire doors are their own assembly ([Fire doors](#fire-doors)). Opening
windows and shutters take the same locks and bars ([Opening windows and
shutters](#opening-windows-and-shutters)), and so do trapdoors and hatches
between levels ([Getting between storeys](#getting-between-storeys)). Every
fitting shows on the door, and examining it lists them and which side each
is on. Ctrl+click routes only pass through a shut door that would open for
you.

### Door fittings

Any door can be fitted with one closing part. Hold the fitting in your active
hand and its tool in the other hand, then click the door; the door must be
open or unlocked. The same tool takes the fitting off again.

| Fitting | Made from | What it does | Tool |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Counterweight closer | 3 rope | Pulls the door shut 6 seconds after it was opened | Hammer |
| Spring closer | 3 iron or bronze | Slams the door shut after 4 seconds | Screwdriver |
| Hydraulic closer | 4 steel | Eases the door shut after 2 to 10 seconds; click it with a screwdriver to set the delay | Screwdriver |
| Spring hinges | 2 iron or bronze | The door swings shut a second after nobody is in the doorway | Screwdriver |

- A door never closes on someone standing in the doorway. It waits until the
  doorway is clear.
- To hold a sprung door open, use grab intent on the open door. It stays open
  while you keep that hand on it and stay next to it. Drop the hold or walk
  away and it shuts.
- A **wedge** (1 wood, or 1 iron) used on an open door with a closer keeps it
  open. Click the door with an empty hand to pull the wedge out, or kick it
  out with harm intent.

### Locks

Locks are separate items. You fit them to a shut door, closet, crate, chest,
basket, jar or other storage. Hold the lock in your active hand and its tool,
if it needs one, in the other hand, then click what you want to lock. A keyed
lock still has its key inside: use the lock in hand to take the key out
before you fit it.

| Lock | Made from | Opens with | Tool to fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wooden pin lock | 3 wood | Its wooden key | Hammer |
| Warded lock | 3 iron | Its iron key; a skeleton key often turns it | Screwdriver |
| Tumbler lock | 3 steel | Its tumbler key only | Screwdriver |
| Spring latch | 4 iron | Its iron key; it locks itself whenever the door shuts | Screwdriver |
| Combination lock | 3 steel | The combination you set | Screwdriver |
| Padlock | 2 iron | Its iron key | None |
| Combination padlock | 2 steel | The combination you set | None |
| Knot seal | 1 rope | Anyone can untie it, but whoever tied it last is remembered | None |
| Clay seal | 1 clay | Stops nobody, but shows when it has been broken | None |
| Hidden catch | 2 wood | Its maker, and anyone who has found it; others must search | Hammer |

Each kind of key only fits its own kind of lock: wooden keys fit pin locks,
iron keys fit warded locks, latches and padlocks, and tumbler keys fit tumbler
locks.

- **Doors:** the keyhole goes on the side you fit the lock from, so fit it from
  outside. From the other side there is a thumb-turn: use the **Lock or
  Unlock** verb, or just open the door, which turns it for you.
- **Copying keys:** use a key on a key blank of the same kind (wood, iron or
  tumbler) to file a copy.
- **Combinations:** use a combination lock or padlock in hand to set its code
  (3 or 4 letters or numbers) before you fit it.
- **Taking a lock off:** use the **Remove Lock** verb with its tool in hand,
  from inside or while it is unlocked.
- **Getting past a lock without its key:**
  - A lockpick works on keyed locks if you are dexterous enough. Pin locks are
    the easiest and tumbler locks the hardest.
  - A skeleton key often turns a warded lock.
  - A knife slipped into the gap, on any intent but harm, pushes back a spring
    latch from outside.
  - Use the **Lock or Unlock** verb on disarm intent to listen to a dial and
    crack its combination. This is slow.
  - Wirecutters on harm intent cut a padlock off, and a knife on harm intent
    cuts through a knot.
  - A crowbar on harm intent prises a padlock off a door, or forces a locked
    chest or crate open. Forcing a container breaks the lock and damages it.
  - Some unusually well-made locks resist all of this.

### Bars, bolts and bracing

| Fitting | Made from | Holds against | Tool to fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bar brackets | 2 wood | - | Hammer |
| Wooden bar | 3 wood | 150 damage | - |
| Iron bar | 3 iron | 400 damage | - |
| Slide bolt | 2 iron | 100 damage | Hammer |

- **Brackets and bolts** go on the side of the door you stand on when you fit
  them. You can have brackets on both sides.
- **Barring:** with the door shut, use a bar on it from the brackets' side to
  drop it in. The door then won't open from either side. Only someone on the
  bar's side can lift it out again, with an empty hand.
- **Bolting:** on the bolt's side, use grab intent on the shut door to shoot
  the bolt, and an empty hand to draw it back.
- **Breaking in:** kicks, blows, rams and siege engines wear down the bars and
  bolt before the door itself. When a bar snaps, the door is free.
- **Bracing:** use grab intent with an empty hand on a shut door to hold it
  shut with your body. Anyone pulling from the other side has to beat your
  strength. Tighten your grip to hold harder, but you tire with every pull.
  Drop the hold to let go.
- **Removing them:** a hammer takes brackets or a bolt off again, from their
  own side, while they are not in use.

### Peep-holes, hatches, mail slots and pet doors

| Feature | Made from | Research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peep-hole | 1 brass | Optics & Instruments |
| Judas hatch | 2 iron | Iron Smithing |
| Mail slot | 1 brass | Bronze Working |
| Pet door | 2 wood | Primitive Construction |

Hold a screwdriver in your other hand and use the feature on the door from
the side it should serve, normally the inside. Every feature on one door
serves the same side. A screwdriver from that side takes one off again.
Windows and shutters don't take them.

- **Peep-hole:** Alt-click the shut door, or right-click it and pick Look
  Through, to see who is outside. Step away to stop. Nobody outside can see
  in.
- **Judas hatch:** right-click the door and pick Slide Hatch. While it is open,
  both sides can see and talk through the shut door.
- **Mail slot:** from outside, use a letter, paper, photo or other tiny item on
  the shut door to post it. It drops inside, or into a closed crate or a bag
  standing there.
- **Pet door:** cats, small dogs, poultry and other small animals can walk
  through the shut door; people can't. Right-click the door and pick Lock Pet
  Door to keep them in or out.

### Fire doors

| Item | Made from | Research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron-clad fire door | 15 iron | Steelmaking |
| Steel fire door | 15 steel | Modern Construction |
| Fusible link | 1 brass | Steelmaking |
| Fire seals | 2 wool cloth | Steelmaking |
| Panic bar | 3 steel | Factories |

- **Fire-rated:** a fire door stops fire for 30 minutes (iron-clad) or an
  hour (steel), but only while it is shut, its closer and seals are whole, and
  it stands between masonry, stone, brick, concrete or metal walls. Examine it
  to see whether it is rated and, if not, why.
- While rated, fire can't spread onto its tile and it takes only a small
  share of the heat. Fire beside it uses up its rating; when that runs out the
  seals burn and it burns like any iron or steel door. Use fire seals on it to
  restore the full rating.
- It shuts itself after every passage, and its closer can't be taken off.
- **Fusible link:** use it on an open door that has a closer to hold the door
  open. When fire comes within two tiles the link melts and the door swings
  shut. An empty hand unhooks it.
- **Panic bar:** hold a screwdriver in your other hand and use the bar on any
  door from the side it should go on. From that side, pushing the door opens
  it even when it is locked or bolted. From the other side it changes nothing.
- Doors burn by what they are made of: wooden doors burn fast, metal doors
  slowly and stone doors hardly at all.

#### Smoke

- A fire fills the room around it with smoke that spreads from tile to tile,
  thinner the further it goes. Walls, shut windows and shut rated fire doors
  stop it, so fire doors keep smoke in the part of the building that is
  burning. An ordinary shut door lets a thin wisp seep round it; an open door
  or open window lets it through.
- Thick smoke blocks sight. Breathing it makes you cough, and thick smoke
  chokes you. A gas mask keeps you breathing.
- Out in the open smoke clears quickly. Indoors it hangs about until the fire
  is out, then slowly thins; open doors and windows to let it out.

### Taking doors down

An unlocked, unbarred or open door can be taken off its hinges in 15 seconds: use a
hammer on wooden, bamboo, bone and stone doors, and a wrench on metal doors,
with any intent but harm. This only works from the inside, where the hinges
are. From outside you have to break the door down. A door with no inside
(an inner door or a gate) can be taken down from either side. You get back
its fittings, its lock, brackets, bolt, panic bar and other features, and about three quarters of its material, less if it
was damaged. Faction doors and blast doors can't be taken down by hand.

### Opening windows and shutters

| Item | Made from | Research | Goes on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Casement window | 4 glass | Glassworking | An empty plain, metal, stone, sandstone, marble or red sandstone frame |
| Wooden shutters | 4 wood | Primitive Construction | Any window frame |
| Iron shutters | 4 iron | Iron Smithing | Any window frame |

Stand in the empty frame to build one. Like a door, you are asked which side is
the inside.

- **Casement:** two glazed sashes that open and shut by hand. Shut, you can see
  through it but nothing gets past. Open, air and thrown things pass, and people
  climb through: walk into it, or drag yourself onto it, and you come out on the
  other side after a moment. A frame with a casement can't also take a fixed
  pane.
- **Shutters:** shut, nobody sees in or out. Open, whatever is behind them
  decides: an empty frame lets people through, glass does not. To glaze a
  shuttered frame, use the glass on the shutters.
- **Locking:** casements and shutters take the same locks, latches, bar
  brackets, bars and slide bolts as doors, with the same inside and outside
  rules (see [Locks](#locks) and [Bars, bolts and bracing](#bars-bolts-and-bracing)).
  A spring latch is the usual window catch. Closers, wedges, fusible links and
  panic bars are only for doors.
- Walking into a shut window or shutters doesn't open them; use your hand.

## Lighting
It is not a proper Civilization until you can see city lights from space!

Electric lights need to be **[connected](Guide_to_Crafting.md#wiring)** to an **[engine](Guide_to_Crafting.md#engines)** to work. **While connecting an electric light to a cable, do not stand on the same tile where the light is. Stand on a neighbouring tile where there is cable on the floor and click the light with the cable coil. This will connect the light to the cable on the floor.**

Remember to restart the engine to power newly connected lights to the existing power network.

A **searchlight** (6 steel, in the **Electrical** menu after **Utilities** research) lights the ground up to six tiles ahead, stopping at walls. It needs no cable. Click it to switch it on or off, and right-click it and choose **Turn Searchlight** to turn it a quarter turn. It is useful for watching a gate or a field at night.
