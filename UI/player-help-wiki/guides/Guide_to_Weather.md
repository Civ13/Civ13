# Weather and Climate

The weather is a living sky. Air of different warmth, damp and pressure lies over the land, lows and highs drift across it with the wind, and storms travel with them. This guide explains how it all fits together, how to read what is coming, and which instruments each age can build to forecast it. For staying alive in it, see [Winter and Long Rounds](Guide_to_Survival.md).

```admonish info
Weather happens wherever there is open sky. Under a roof and underground none of it reaches you, though you still feel the cold.
```

## Climate and seasons

Every land has its own climate: temperate, coast, taiga, tundra, semi-arid, desert, savanna or jungle. The climate sets how warm each season is, how big the swing is between day and night, and how damp the air usually is. The [table of temperatures](Guide_to_Survival.md#how-cold-it-gets) gives each land's mid-winter and mid-summer.

- **Through the year** temperatures change smoothly, coldest in mid-winter and warmest in mid-summer.
- **Through the day** it is coldest at early morning and warmest in the afternoon. Water warms and cools slowly, so the coast and the sea swing less than inland. Cloud and rain bring day and night closer together.
- **High ground** is colder: about 6.5 C for every kilometre you climb.
- **The south** has its seasons the other way round, and each land keeps its own [local time](Guide_to_Survival.md#time-zones-and-seasons-across-the-lands).

## The air

Over the land lies a patchwork of air, about 16 tiles across each, and each patch has four things that change by the minute:

| What | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Temperature** | how warm the air is, what a thermometer reads |
| **Humidity** | how damp the air is, from bone-dry desert to steaming jungle. The **dew point** is how far the air would have to cool to start wetting the ground |
| **Pressure** | the weight of the air, about **1013 hPa** on an ordinary day. Below 1000 is stormy, above 1020 is settled |
| **Cloud** | how much of the sky is covered. Clouds keep the nights milder and the days cooler |

Neighbouring patches blend into one another, so the weather changes gradually as you walk.

## Lows, highs and fronts

The big weather comes from **lows** and **highs**, wide areas of low and high pressure that form at the edge of the land and drift across it with the wind, more slowly than the wind itself. A low lasts from half an hour to an hour.

- **A low** brings unsettled weather. Storms gather along its front, a little ahead of its heart. The air ahead of a low turns milder, and colder air follows behind it. A falling barometer is the first sign that one is coming.
- **A high** brings settled, clear weather. In summer a strong high can stall and turn into a **heat wave**.
- **Thunderstorms** can also build on their own on a hot, humid afternoon. They bring heavy rain and lightning, and blow themselves out in a quarter of an hour or so.

Lows, highs and storms do not stop at the edge of a land. Neighbouring lands and oceans share them, so a storm you watch roll off the map carries on into the next region.

## Wind

Wind blows from high pressure toward low, turned by the turning of the world: north of the equator it circles a low anticlockwise, and south of it clockwise. On top of that:

- **A storm** overhead blows a strong wind, and a severe one a gale.
- **Along a coast** a sea breeze blows in off the water on sunny afternoons, and a land breeze blows out to sea on clear nights.
- **Hills** are windier on top and sheltered in the valleys, valleys turn the wind along their floor, and on clear nights cold air drains down the slopes.

The wind can come from any of eight directions and turns an eighth of the way at a time. Sailors, windmills and anyone out in the cold feel it; see [Sailing and the wind](Guide_to_Boats.md#sailing-and-the-wind).

| Beaufort force | Wind | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 to 2 | calm to a light breeze | fog can form on a clear night |
| 3 to 4 | a gentle to moderate breeze | no new fog, and a moderate breeze blows fog away |
| 5 to 6 | a fresh to strong breeze | a strong wind: sails can tear, and a plain canoe stays ashore on salt water |
| 7 and up | a near gale or worse | a gale: furl the sails |

## Rain, snow, sleet and hail

What falls from a storm depends on how cold the air is where you stand, not on the season's name:

- **Rain** above about 3 C, **sleet** just above freezing, and **snow** below it. A winter storm may snow at night and rain on a mild afternoon, and hilltops may get snow while the valley gets rain.
- **Freezing rain** falls when milder air arrives ahead of a low over frozen ground. It glazes the ground with ice for about ten minutes.
- **Hail** falls from the heart of a violent summer thunderstorm, and hurts unless you wear a helmet.
- **Hills** catch more rain and snow on the side facing the wind; the far side lies in a drier **rain shadow**.
- **In the desert** a storm raises sand instead.

## Fog, dew and frost

On a clear, still night the ground cools under the open sky. When it cools to the dew point:

- **dew** settles on the ground, and an open barrel collects it;
- if it is near freezing, **frost** whitens the ground instead and can nip young crops (see [Frost](Guide_to_Farming.md#frost));
- in damp air **fog** forms, thickest in the valleys. It lingers into the morning and burns off by midday. A breeze or rain stops it.

Over the sea, damp air can lie as **sea fog** even by day while the wind is light. Deserts rarely see fog at all. See [Fog, mist, dew and frost](Guide_to_Survival.md#fog-mist-dew-and-frost) for how far you can see in it.

## How the air feels

Your body warms or cools toward what the air **feels** like, not what the thermometer says. Out of doors:

- **wind chill** makes cold air colder;
- **humid heat** stops sweat cooling you;
- **the sun** adds a few degrees on a clear afternoon.

A windproof coat keeps off the wind, and a brimmed hat or light robe keeps off the sun. See [Wind, humidity and sun](Guide_to_Survival.md#wind-humidity-and-sun).

## Reading the weather ahead

Each age can build better instruments, and the better the instrument, the further and surer its forecast. Instruments that stand on the ground only work out of doors.

| Instrument | Made from | Research | What it tells you | How far ahead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Weather vane** | 6 soft wood | Primitive Construction | where the wind is from, how strong, and whether the sky upwind is dark | no time, only "it is coming" |
| **Rain gauge** | 3 bronze | Bronze Working | how many mm of rain, or melted snow, fell since you last tipped it out | none: it records |
| **Thermometer** | 1 glass and quicksilver | Optics & Instruments | the air's temperature, and outdoors what it feels like | none |
| **Barometer** | 8 iron and quicksilver | Optics & Instruments | the pressure in hPa and whether it is rising or falling | a quarter of an hour, to about five minutes |
| **Hygrometer** | 2 soft wood | Optics & Instruments | the humidity and dew point | tonight's dew, frost or fog |
| **Anemometer** | 6 iron | Steam Power | the wind in m/s, km/h and Beaufort force, and its chill | none |
| **Weather station** | 15 soft wood | Electricity | everything above, from one screen, plus a log of the day | half an hour, to the minute, with what will fall and the lows and highs coming in |

How to use them:

- **Hand instruments** (thermometer, barometer, hygrometer): use them in your hand. The barometer and thermometer need quicksilver poured in first; see [Quicksilver Crafts](Guide_to_Quicksilver_Crafts.md#barometer-and-thermometer).
- **Standing instruments** (weather vane, rain gauge, anemometer, weather station): build them outdoors and examine them. An empty hand tips out a rain gauge, and opens a weather station's log. The log gets one line as each part of the day begins and keeps the last twelve.
- **The [farmer's almanac](Guide_to_Farming.md#farmers-almanac)** gives the season's typical temperature and a short outlook.

**Reading the signs:**

- A **falling** barometer means a low or a storm is coming, and **falling fast** means a severe one.
- A **rising** barometer means it is clearing.
- A **damp evening with a clear sky** means dew, frost or fog by morning.
- A **dark sky upwind** means rain is on its way, even without instruments.

A forecast supposes the wind holds. If the wind shifts, a storm can turn away from you, or toward you.

## Tips

- Farmers: watch the hygrometer on clear autumn and spring evenings, and cover tender crops when it promises frost.
- Sailors: a falling barometer and a rising wind mean it is time to reef or make for harbour.
- Travellers: in fog, keep to roads you know, and wait for the midday sun to burn it off.
- Rain barrels and crops are only watered where the rain actually falls, so a rain gauge shows you which fields got it.
