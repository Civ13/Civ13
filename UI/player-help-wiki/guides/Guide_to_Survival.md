# Winter and Long Rounds

New here? Start with the six-line [first night list](Guide_to_Your_First_Day.md#your-first-night): fire, shelter, water, food, warmth and people. This page covers the harder weather that comes after.

## Staying warm

Cover exposed body parts and wear a coat over ordinary clothing. Overlapping layers add insulation, while shorts and loincloths protect only the parts they cover. Ordinary clothing's cold rating is **240 K**, about **-33 C**; it stops insulating below that temperature. Choose suitable cold-weather equipment for extreme climates.

Rain and immersion make you wet, weakening clothing insulation and increasing cooling. See [Rain and wet clothes](#rain-and-wet-clothes) for gear that keeps you dry. Standing under a pitched canopy or a lean-to keeps the rain off and blocks the wind, so in cold weather it feels up to 5 C warmer, though never warmer than 15 C. A nearby lit brazier, campfire, iron furnace or fireplace provides warmth; out in the open a fire only warms you within two tiles. Indoors, how warm you stay depends on the room: a roof alone only breaks the wind, while a closed room with good walls holds its warmth and a fire inside heats the whole room. See [Keeping a building warm](Guide_to_Construction.md#keeping-a-building-warm). Warmth dries you faster and helps recovery from hypothermia; shelter alone can still be cold.

Complete coverage buys time but does not make you immune to cold. A dangerously cold body takes harm even when fully covered. Seek warmth before exposure becomes severe, and account for other injuries and hunger.

### How cold it gets

Each land has its own climate. Temperatures change smoothly through the year: the coldest point is the middle of winter, the warmest is the middle of summer, and spring and fall fall in between. Every day is coldest at early morning and warmest in the afternoon. Under rain or snow the day and night are closer together.

| Land | Mid-winter | Mid-summer | Day to night |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Temperate | about -2 C | about 20 C | 10 C |
| Coast | about 5 C | about 17 C | 5 C |
| Taiga | about -18 C | about 15 C | 12 C |
| Tundra | about -28 C | about 8 C | 8 C |
| Semi-arid | about 5 C | about 26 C | 15 C |
| Desert | about 13 C | about 34 C | 18 C |
| Savanna | about 22 C | about 28 C | 12 C |
| Jungle | about 26 C | about 28 C | 8 C |

On lands with a wet and a dry season, the dry season is the hotter one. These are averages: half the "day to night" figure is added in the afternoon and taken off at dawn, so a desert summer runs from about 25 C at dawn to 43 C in the afternoon. Rain makes the air a little colder. A blizzard is about 10 C colder than the season, and a heat wave about 6 C hotter. High ground is colder too: a few degrees on a hill, and up to about 10 C on the highest ground. The [farmer's almanac](Guide_to_Farming.md#farmers-almanac) lists the typical temperature of each season where you stand.

### Wind, humidity and sun

Out of doors you feel more than the air's temperature, and your body warms or cools toward what you feel:

- **Wind chill.** When the air is 10 C or colder, wind carries your warmth away. A cold morning at -2 C in a strong wind feels like about -10 C. A coat, cloak or rain gear over your clothes keeps most of the wind off: an ordinary suit stops a little, a coat about half, fur and leather coats more, and oilskins and rain coats most of it. Indoors and under a roof there is no wind chill.
- **Humid heat.** When the air is 27 C or hotter and damp, sweat stops cooling you, so a muggy 32 C afternoon feels like about 40 C. Dry heat does not do this.
- **The sun.** On a clear midday or afternoon at 20 C or warmer, the sun adds about 3 C. A brimmed or draped hat, such as a straw hat, rice hat, sombrero, cowboy hat, pith helmet, keffiyeh or turban, shades you, and a light robe, tunic or toga shades you more. A loose robe also lets out some of the humid heat. Shade under a roof or an open umbrella keeps the sun off.

When the wind, humid heat or sun is getting to you, you are told so, and what would help. A thermometer still reads the air itself.

## Storms

For how lows, highs, wind and fog work together, see [Weather and Climate](Guide_to_Weather.md).

Weather comes as storms that travel across the land with the wind. Rain, snow or blowing sand falls only under the storm, so one valley can be soaked while the next is dry, and you can walk out from under a storm or wait for it to pass.

Storms do not come from nowhere. Areas of low pressure drift across the land with the wind, and storms gather along their fronts: the air ahead of a low turns milder, and colder air follows behind it. High pressure brings settled, clear weather, and a strong high in summer can stall into a heat wave. On hot, humid afternoons a thunderstorm can also build suddenly overhead, with heavy rain and lightning, and blow itself out in a quarter of an hour or so.

Weather does not stop at the edge of a land. Neighbouring lands and oceans share the same lows, highs and storms, so a storm you watch roll off the edge of the map carries on into the next region, and you can sail or walk on into it. In the middle latitudes, such as Europe, Asia and North America, weather usually moves from west to east. In the tropics and near the poles it usually moves from east to west. On Gaia the big lows and highs come in from the surrounding ocean, but the island's storms are its own.

- **Seeing one coming.** If a storm is heading your way while you are outdoors, you are told which direction it is coming from. The cloud's shadow darkens the ground a little before the rain reaches you.
- **What falls** depends on the land and on how cold the air is where you stand. In the desert a storm raises sand. Elsewhere it rains when the air is above about 3 C, falls as sleet just above freezing, and snows when it is colder. So a winter storm may snow at night and rain on a mild afternoon, and hilltops get snow while the valley gets rain. A storm is light at its edge and heaviest in the middle.
- **Freezing rain.** When the ground is below freezing but milder air is arriving ahead of a low, rain falls and freezes where it lands. It leaves the open ground icy for about ten minutes, longer while it stays cold. Running on it can make you slip unless your boots grip ice well.
- **Hail.** The heart of a violent summer thunderstorm can hail. Hailstones hurt your head, so take cover or wear a helmet; any helmet or hard hat stops them.
- **Hills** catch more rain and snow on the side the wind blows against, and the far side lies in a drier rain shadow.
- **Severe storms** have a core of extreme weather: a blizzard in cold lands, a monsoon in the jungle, a sandstorm in the desert. Get under a roof before the core arrives. Blizzards and sandstorms pass on with the storm.
- **Heat waves** settle over a place for a while and move on. You get thirstier, crops need more water and there is no dew. You are told when one arrives and when it has passed.
- **Lightning and thunder.** Heavy rain storms bring lightning. You see the sky flash and hear the thunder a moment later, later the further away it struck. Lightning does not hurt you or start fires.
- **Wind** rises under a storm and can blow an open umbrella inside out. The wind can blow from any of eight directions. It shifts an eighth of a turn at a time and never swings right round at once.

**Reading the weather ahead.** Each age has better instruments, and the better the instrument, the further and surer its forecast. The [Weather and Climate](Guide_to_Weather.md#reading-the-weather-ahead) guide has the full table.

- A **weather vane** (6 soft wood, Primitive Construction) stands on the ground outdoors and swings to point into the wind. Examine it to learn where the wind is from, how strong it is, and whether the sky upwind is dark.
- A **rain gauge** (3 bronze, Bronze Working) catches what falls, in mm.
- A **barometer** (8 iron, Optics & Instruments) reads the pressure in hPa and whether it is rising or falling, and tells you about how many minutes until weather breaks or passes, up to a quarter of an hour ahead. A falling needle means weather is coming; falling fast, a severe storm.
- A **thermometer** reads the air and what it feels like, and a **hygrometer** (2 soft wood, Optics & Instruments) reads the damp and warns of dew, frost or fog tonight.
- An **anemometer** (6 iron, Steam Power) counts the wind in m/s and on the Beaufort scale.
- A **weather station** (15 soft wood, Electricity) reads everything at once, forecasts half an hour ahead with what will fall, and keeps a log of the day.
- The **farmer's almanac** has an Outlook line with the same forecast.

A forecast supposes the wind holds. If the wind shifts, the storm can turn away, or toward you. On the lands of Earth and the open oceans, storms hold their course, so a forecast there is steadier.

Roofs keep all of it off. Rain barrels fill, crops are watered and fires are doused only where the rain is actually falling.

## Fog, mist, dew and frost

The air holds more moisture in some lands than others, and it keeps that moisture as it cools at night.

- **Fog and mist.** On a clear, still night in a humid land the air cools until it can hold no more water, and fog forms on the ground. It settles thickest in valleys, lingers into the morning and burns off by midday. A breeze keeps it from forming, and rain clears it. Over the sea and along the shore, damp air can lie as sea fog even by day while the wind is light. Dry lands such as deserts rarely see any.
- **Seeing in fog.** Out of doors, mist hazes everything beyond about 5 tiles. In thick fog only the nearest 4 or 5 tiles stay clear, and nothing past about 9 can be seen. At night the fog is dark. Under a roof there is none. At sea, fog and mist shorten a lookout's range too.
- **Dew.** On a clear, calm night the ground cools and dew settles on it. An open barrel left outdoors with nothing overhead collects it, and a little more in fog, but a windy or cloudy night brings none.
- **Frost.** On the same clear, calm nights, when the air is near freezing, frost whitens the ground instead. It can nip back young crops; see the [farming guide](Guide_to_Farming.md#frost) for covering them.

## Rain and wet clothes

Standing in the open during rain slowly soaks you, faster in heavy rain or a monsoon. Being indoors, under a pitched canopy or under a lean-to keeps the rain off entirely. Wet clothing insulates less, and on settlement maps soaked clothes also slow you down.

**Rain gear.** Your coat or cape, your hat and any cape fastened over your clothes each keep off part of the rain, and they stack. Ordinary clothes help a little; dedicated gear helps a lot. No combination keeps you perfectly dry forever.

| Gear | Made from | Research | Keeps off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lean-to (shelter) | wood | Primitive Construction | all rain while you are under it |
| Straw rain cape | leaves | Weaving | 70% |
| Oiled paper umbrella | bamboo | Papermaking | 70% while open |
| Sou'wester (hat) | cloth | Haberdashery | 50% |
| Oilskin coat | cloth | Textile Mills | 85% |
| Black umbrella | cloth | Textile Mills | 80% while open |
| Folding umbrella | plastic | Synthetic Fabrics | 85% while open |

Fur coats, ponchos, capes and straw hats also shed some rain. Examine a garment to see how well it keeps rain out.

**Umbrellas.** Use an umbrella in hand to open or close it. Held open in either hand, it keeps most rain off, and in hot sun it gives shade that makes the heat a few degrees easier to bear. A gale can blow an open umbrella inside out; use it in hand to bend it back.

**Greasing clothes.** Use a container holding at least 5 units of lard, olive oil or corn oil on a coat, hat or cape to rub the grease in. Lard makes it shed 60% of the rain, and plant oil 45%, until enough rain has washed the grease out.

**Capes over clothing.** Use a cape, such as the straw rain cape or a red or blue cape, on your uniform, coat or armor to fasten it over that piece. A fastened cape adds its rain protection and warmth. Remove it with the Remove Accessory verb to get the same cape back. A coat or suit of armor holds one cape.

**Drying off.** Out of the rain you dry slowly in the cold and faster in warm air. Standing within two tiles of a lit fire dries you about three times faster. The **Wring Out Clothes** verb (IC tab) takes a few seconds and leaves soaked clothes only damp. It does nothing while you are still standing in water.

**Heavy armor.** Plate armor and heavy body armor slow you down. Each ballistic plate slid into a plate carrier adds a little more weight.
## Footwear for the ground and weather

What is on your feet changes how fast you cross snow, mud and water, and how well you keep your footing. Examine a pair of shoes to see what it is good for.

| Footwear | Made from | Good for | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Snowshoes | Wood (Stone Age) | Walk on top of deep snow at nearly full speed; warm | Clumsy on bare ground, poor in mud, unsteady on steep slopes |
| Mukluks | Leather (Stone Age) | Faster in snow, warm, fair grip on ice | Soak up mud and water |
| Hobnailed boots | Leather (Iron Smithing) | Never slip on ice, sure footing on steep slopes, keep feet dry | A little slower than light shoes |
| Pattens | Wood (Tailoring) | Lift you clear of mud and puddles, keep feet dry | Wobbly on slopes, no grip on ice |
| Rubber boots | Plastic or rubber (Ready-Made Clothing) | Best in mud and wading, keep feet dry | Little grip on ice, not insulated |
| Mountaineering boots | Steel (Alloys) | Crampons never slip on ice, best on steep slopes, good in snow, warm | Heavy |

Older footwear counts too. Fur, wool and winter boots are warm and help in snow. Leather, riding, work and military boots shed water and help a little in mud. Geta lift you out of mud. Sandals, flip-flops and slippers are light, but in snow your toes freeze as if barefoot.

**Ice.** Running on frozen water can make you slip and fall. Walking never does. Boots with a good grip halve the chance, and hobnails or crampons stop it entirely.

## Breaking and melting ice

**Breaking ice.** On harm intent, a pickaxe breaks a tile of ice into open water in about 12 seconds. A hatchet or crowbar takes about 15 seconds and a chisel about 25. Strong workers and miners are quicker. A crowbar breaks ice on any intent. Bare hands on harm intent only get through **thin ice**, and tools break thin ice in half the time. Stand on the next tile or the shore, not on the ice you are breaking. On any other intent an axe, pick or chisel cuts an ice-fishing hole instead (see [Fishing](Guide_to_Fishing.md#ice-fishing)).

**Thin ice.** Ice is thin when it has newly frozen over, when it has been salted, or when the air above it is above freezing and it is rotting. Examining ice tells you whether it is thin. Thin ice gives way under carts, vehicles, ridden animals and anyone dragging something or carrying something heavy. If the water under it is deep, you will have to swim.

**Freezing and thawing.** On Gaia and the other regions, ice comes and goes with the weather:

- In hard frost, open water freezes out from the shore or from ice already there, about a tile every ten minutes. Still, shallow water freezes first, at about -2 C. The shallow sea needs about -6 C, deep water -8 C and the deep sea -15 C. Rivers with a current need about 4 C more cold.
- New ice is thin. It bears like any other ice after about an hour of frost.
- In a thaw, ice melts back from its edges, and thin ice melts quickly.
- A tile you broke open skins over with thin ice again after about half an hour of frost.

**Keeping water open.** Scatter a handful of salt from a salt pile or salt shaker on ice to break it up into open water. Scatter it on open water to keep that water from freezing for about two hours. Salt does nothing when the air is -12 C or colder. A channel boats keep using stays open, and so does water with a boat or a person in it.

**Cold, wet feet.** When you are already cold, standing in water or mud chills your feet unless your footwear is waterproof, and snow freezes bare or open-toed feet.

**Hills.** Running down a steep slope can trip you. Hobnails and crampons make that much less likely; snowshoes and pattens make it more likely.

## Clearing snow

Fallen snow on grass can be scooped up with a shovel into a snow pile, leaving the grass underneath. Once the snow is gone, a shovel digs up the grass as usual.

## Time zones and seasons across the lands

All the lands of Earth and Gaia share one clock and one calendar, so they stay in step. A day lasts 2 real hours: Early Morning, Morning, Midday, Afternoon, Evening and Night are 20 minutes each.

Each land keeps its own local time, one hour for every 15 degrees east or west:

| Land | Local time |
| --- | --- |
| Pacific Ocean | 11 hours behind |
| North America | 7 hours behind |
| South America | 4 hours behind |
| Greenland | 3 hours behind |
| Gaia, Atlantic Ocean | 2 hours behind |
| Antarctica, Arctic Ocean, Southern Ocean | the reference time |
| Europe, Africa | 1 hour ahead |
| Indian Ocean | 5 hours ahead |
| Asia | 6 hours ahead |
| Oceania | 9 hours ahead |

So when it is Midday in Asia, it can still be Early Morning on Gaia. If you travel far east or west, you may arrive in a different part of the day.

The seasons are opposite in the south. When the northern lands are in summer, South America, Oceania, Antarctica, the Indian Ocean and the Southern Ocean are in winter, and the other way round. Spring in the north is fall in the south.

## The sun and shadows

On the continents the sun crosses the sky through the day. It rises in the east, stands highest at Midday and sets in the west. Daylight is warm and golden while the sun is low and white when it is high.

Trees cast shadows on the ground away from the sun: long at dawn and dusk, short at noon. On Gaia, hills and ridges cast shadows too, and the slopes facing the sun are lighter than those facing away.

How high the sun climbs depends on where you are and the season:
- **Summer:** the sun is high and shadows are short.
- **Winter:** the sun stays low and shadows are long, even at noon.
- **Hemisphere:** in the northern lands the noon sun is in the south, so shadows point north. In South America it is in the north, so shadows point south. Near the equator, in the Jungle, it is almost overhead.

At night there is no sun and no tree shadows.

## Fighting fires

A fire burns its tile for a short while, hurts and ignites anyone standing in it, and keeps spreading to neighbouring grass and wooden floors until it is put out or runs out of things to burn. Bare dirt, sand, stone and water do not carry it. Rain helps put fires out. Act early: one burning tile is easy to stop, a long burning front is not.

- **Fire beater**: a leafy branch made from 2 wood with Primitive Construction knowledge. Hold it and click a fire next to you to beat at it. About half the swings put the fire out.
- **Fire flapper**: a hide flap on a pole made from 1 leather with the same knowledge. It works the same way with better odds and lasts about three times as long.
- **Soak it first**: click open water, a well, or an open container holding water with the beater or flapper in hand. A wet tool rarely fails and wears far more slowly, but it dries out after several swings, so soak it again. A dry tool scorches and falls apart quickly.
- **Someone is burning**: click them with the beater or flapper (not on harm intent) to beat the flames off. A burning person can also drop and roll with Resist.
- **Nothing to hand**: a pelt, leather or cloth held in your hand can smother a fire when you click it. It often fails, and a piece may scorch away.
- **Water**: click a fire with an open container to empty its water onto it. The more water, the surer: a cupful rarely works, half a bucket always does, and a full bucket also splashes the fires on the tiles around it and puts out anyone burning on that tile. Refill at a well or open water and keep a line of buckets moving.
- **Wet blanket**: soak a bedsheet in open water, a well or a container of water. Thrown over a fire it usually smothers it, and wrapped around a burning person it always puts them out. One soaking lasts four uses. A dry sheet works poorly and may burn up.
- **Fire hook**: a long pole with an iron hook, made from 3 iron and a wood handle with Iron Tools knowledge. Click a thatch or timber roof, wall or floor, or a bush or tall grass, to pull it down and leave a gap the fire cannot cross. Clicking the ground under a roof reaches for the roof. With a fire within three tiles this takes a few seconds; with no fire near it takes three times as long. A wall or floor that was not yet burning gives back half its material. Stone, brick, tile and trees cannot be pulled down.
- **Hand pump fire engine**: a water tub on wheels with a pump and a short hose, built from 20 wood with Wagons & Ships knowledge. Pour water into it from buckets (it holds 400 units), pull it close to the fire, then click it with an empty hand to take the nozzle. Click where the water should land, up to five tiles away. Each jet uses 20 water, puts out the fire on the tile it lands on and anyone burning there, and often the fires on the tiles around it. Walls stop the jet. The nozzle reaches three tiles from the engine: go further, drop it or have the engine pulled away and it returns to the engine. Click the engine with the nozzle to hang it back, and examine the engine to see how many jets are left.
- **Fire hose reel**: a hose drum built from 10 steel with Machined Tools knowledge. Build it right beside a sink or a well, which is where its water comes from; a puddle or a dried-up sink will not do. Take the nozzle and spray exactly as with the hand pump engine. The hose reaches seven tiles from the reel, and it keeps spraying for as long as the sink or well has water.
- **Fire extinguisher**: made from 7 steel with Machined Tools knowledge, and empty until you fill it with water at a sink or well. Hold it with your other hand free and click toward the fire: each burst puts out every fire and every burning person or animal on the three tiles in front of you, and walls stop it. A full one holds twelve bursts; examine it to see how many are left.

Stand beside the fire, never in it, and work along the edge that is moving toward what you want to save.

## Smoke signals

A lit campfire or fire pit under the open sky can send smoke signals. Hold leather or cloth and click the burning fire: one piece burns away and a column of smoke rises over the fire for a few seconds. Everyone awake and outdoors on the same level within 300 tiles is told which way the smoke is and roughly how far, from close by to very far off. People indoors do not see it, and a fire under a roof cannot send one. A fire on a roof is seen 100 tiles further for each storey it stands above the ground, and people outdoors on the ground below see it as well. After each signal the fire needs about half a minute before the next. Anyone can read the smoke, friend or foe, so agree on what your signals mean beforehand. The fire tells you whether anyone was outdoors to see your signal, and examining a lit fire under the open sky reminds you how to send one.

### Inviting newcomers

On the mainland, each smoke signal also asks whether you want to **invite newcomers**. Anyone can say yes; you do not need a faction. For the next 30 minutes new characters can then choose to start a short walk from your fire, and you are told when one is on the way and from which direction. Signal again to keep the invitation going.

The invitation only holds while the fire stays lit under the open sky and someone awake stays within about 15 tiles of it. It ends at once if the fire goes out, is roofed over, moved or dismantled. Newcomers arrive as nomads, so greet them and recruit them in person. **Check Faction List** marks factions whose fire is calling.

## Abandoned debris

Rubbish and empty spent casings left lying around rot away after **4 season changes**, and you can see a warning when you examine them after **2**. Where the seasons do not change, the warning comes after **6 hours** and they rot away after **12**.

Picking up or moving debris resets its clock. Things kept in bags, backpacks, ammunition boxes and closed closets or crates are safe. Useful items, such as tools, crops, containers, vehicles, money, loaded ammunition and arrows or bolts, never rot away.

For dead bodies, see [Human bodies and remains](Guide_to_Medical.md#human-bodies-and-remains).
