# Books and Scholars

On persistent worlds, knowledge is written down instead of handed out. Nobody can craft anything they haven't studied, research is written into books, and the world only leaves the Stone Age when someone discovers the way out. This guide covers studying, researching, writing and copying books, and the scholar's trade.

```admonish info
This applies to persistent worlds such as [North and South America](../maps/The_Americas.md) and the other lands connected to them. Round maps such as [Civilizations & Nomads](../gamemodes/Civilizations_and_Nomads.md) still use the faction [Research](Guide_to_Research.md) tree. The node names, prerequisites and prototypes in that guide's [node reference](Guide_to_Research.md#full-node-reference) are the same here.
```

## Getting started

Each land starts in 5000 B.C. and has no clock: it moves into a new age only when someone there finishes a **capstone** chapter, or a copy of one is brought in and kept there (the prototype nodes, such as Bronze Working), and every player is told when that happens.

1. **Found a faction.** The founder receives the **Stone Age master book**, which holds every Stone Age chapter, and a **writing slate**. Nobody knows those chapters yet.
2. **Study the chapters you need.** Hold the book, use it and press **Study** beside a chapter. Each chapter needs its prerequisites first, but only those: you can skip every chapter that the ones you want do not build on. See [what needs what](Guide_to_Research.md#what-needs-what).
3. **Craft what you've learned.** Crafting checks your own knowledge only. Your faction's research and the world's age don't unlock anything for you.
4. **Research new chapters** at a research bench, then study them like any other chapter.

**Pencils, paper and blank books** can be crafted by anyone from the start, with no knowledge needed. They are made from wooden planks, which anyone can cut from logs with an axe or by hand. For a new character's first steps, see [Your First Day](Guide_to_Your_First_Day.md). **Parchment** is cut from a dry hide with a sharp tool (four sheets per hide) and works like paper. In the Stone Age a sheet of paper is called **papyrus**; it is the same sheet. Sheets clipped together in a paper bundle can be used straight from the bundle. See [Parchment](../Miscellaneous_Crafting.md#parchment) for the whole hide route.

## Studying

Studying a chapter takes 1 minute in the Stone Age plus 30 seconds for each later age, and you keep it until you die. You can study from a book in hand, from a loose chapter page, or from a book lying on a research bench. Studying needs no scholarship, only the chapter's prerequisites.

You can stop studying at any time: walk away, put the book down or get interrupted, and the part you already read is kept. Next time, the chapter shows how far you got (for example **Continue: Iron Tools (40% read)**) and studying it only takes the time left. Your progress belongs to you, not the book, so you can continue from any copy of the chapter, after a restart or in another region. Recipe manuals work the same way.

Using a research book in hand opens its window. Alt-click does the same for a book lying next to you. Its chapters are sorted for you: first the ones you can study now (**Ready to study**), then the ones whose prerequisites you still need (**Needs groundwork**), then the ones you already know (**Known**). Each row shows the age, the discipline, how long studying takes and, when it is locked, which chapters to learn first. Type in **Find a chapter** to search by chapter, recipe or discipline, use the buttons under it to show one group, and click a row to see its groundwork and the recipes it teaches. Examining a book shows the same three groups. Recipe compendiums sort their chapters the same way.

**Death clears everything you know**, both chapters and scholarship. For one day a new character can take most of it back by starting near where you died, beside your faction's recruitment station or at a bed you had held for a day (see [If you die](Guide_to_Your_First_Day.md#if-you-die)). Otherwise a new character starts with nothing, so keep your books safe and your scholars alive.

**Can't study a chapter?** Check **Needs groundwork** for missing prerequisites. You'll also need enough light and at least Broken proficiency in the chapter's language. If you can't read a coded copy, look for a readable one. You do not need a scholarship level just to read.

## Research benches

A **research bench** costs 8 soft wood. Benches belong to nobody: anyone can use any bench, and a bench holds only the work in progress.

1. Put a book on the bench, click a subject in the bench window and press **Research this here**. The research is written into that book. Take the book away and the unfinished work goes with it.
2. Stay near the bench. It makes progress while someone who knows every prerequisite of the subject is within three tiles and in sight of it, with a writing tool (see [The scholar's trade](#the-scholars-trade)). Beside the bench the work runs at full pace; two or three tiles off it runs at three quarters, so you can craft, cook or build close by while it goes on.
   - To keep both hands free, click the bench with your pencil, pen or writing slate to rest it there. Right-click the bench and choose **Take Writing Tool** to pick it up again.
3. Speed it up by using **samples** on the bench: existing examples of things the subject unlocks. The first two kinds of sample you take apart for a chapter are worth the most, a fifth of the chapter each. Capstones also need their **prototype** before the chapter is finished, or from Steam Power on a [demonstration](#demonstrations), and from Steam Power on a [bill of materials](#bills-of-materials).
4. When the research is done, the chapter is written into the book. **It teaches nobody**, not even the researcher. Whoever wants it still has to study it.

The first time anyone finishes a capstone chapter, the land they are in moves into the next age. Other lands follow only when a copy of the capstone is carried there (see [Epochs](Guide_to_Epochs.md)). Two ages have a peaceful capstone as well as a military one: the Printing Press opens the same age as Gunpowder, and Powered Flight opens the same age as Mechanized Warfare. Either one is enough.

### Moving or taking down a bench

Use a **wrench on Harm intent** to unfasten the bench, move it, then use the wrench again to fasten it. Use a **hammer on Harm intent** only if you want to dismantle it: this returns one wood and drops any books and writing tools left on it. On maps with faction research, only members of the faction that owns the bench can do this.

### Learning by doing

You don't have to wait at the bench for the whole time. From the Stone Age on, things you do out in the world also count as research. Do the work, then stand at a bench that is researching the subject, and your experience is written into the book. Each book takes each piece of work once, and the subject's details in the bench window show how far along you are.

- **Deeds.** Every subject up to the Industrial Age that builds on earlier ones asks for two or three particular pieces of work that use what you already know. Cooperage wants two tool handles, a set of wooden dowels and a workbench; Brickmaking wants a lime kiln, a mason's saw and three rough stone walls; Wagons & Ships wants a raft, a cart and a carriage; Factories wants a steam engine, a pole lathe and a toolbox. Together a subject's deeds are worth half of it. The bench window lists them by name under **Without waiting**, and each time you finish one the game tells you it counted.
- **Foraging** advances Basic Toolmaking, Primitive Construction, Pottery & Cookware, Weaving, Herbalism and Basic Agriculture.
- **Butchering** animals advances Stone Arms, Archery and Primitive Armor.
- **Mining, smelting and firing** advance the Natural Science subjects. Each ore vein you strike while mining counts toward Early Chemical Cells, Gunpowder and Galvanic Cells. Each ore or metal in a bloomery, blast furnace, kiln or tatara that you light counts toward Steelmaking. Each piece of unfired clay or load of glass sand in an oven or fireplace that you light counts toward Glassworking and Optics & Instruments. Each of these is worth a small share of the subject.

- **Prototypes.** Once the unfinished chapter in your book reaches **34%** (rounded up to a whole research point), you can make eligible recipes before learning the chapter, as long as you know its prerequisites. Take the book off the bench and hold it in hand, not inside a bag. Use the recipe's normal materials, tools and workstation. The first five successful builds for that subject in that book each add progress. After that the book is still your reference, and you can keep making the subject's things from it for as long as you like. Working from a book is slow: the first piece takes twice as long as usual. Each piece you make that way makes the next a little quicker, down to one and a half times as long. Once you finish the chapter and read it, you work at full speed. Making something beside a teacher who knows the subject is never slowed. The subject's things stay listed in the crafting window while a book you carry is working on it. If you can't make one yet, you are told what is missing: the subjects it builds on, or how many points the book still needs.
- **Trial and error.** You don't have to wait for that 34% threshold. Hold or carry a book and use **Attempt a Subject** (Object tab) to open it to a subject whose groundwork you know. While you carry the book you can try to make that subject's things from an empty chapter, in the crafting menu, at an anvil or at a workbench.
  - Most early attempts fail. A failure uses up the materials, leaves a ruined piece and writes a lesson into the book worth a small share of the chapter. A book takes five lessons per subject.
  - An empty chapter gives about 15 chances in 100. Every bit of the chapter already written raises that, each earlier failure on the same thing adds 10, and scholarship above what the subject needs adds up to 15. Too little scholarship takes up to 10 away.
  - A working piece counts as a prototype, and you keep it. Like any work from a book, it takes longer than usual. You can make five early pieces of a subject this way; after that, get the chapter to the ordinary 34% threshold.
  - From the Industrial Age on, a failed attempt can burn, sicken or cut you a little, so don't try it wounded.
- **Field study.** Hold paper, parchment or a book with a pen or pencil in your other hand, stand next to a building, machine or tool that a subject makes, and use **Study in the Field** (Object tab). After about half a minute your notes are written into the book, or onto the sheet as field notes to use on a bench researching that subject. It works on anyone's things, not only your own. A book takes three different kinds of thing per subject, each once.

- **Record a find.** Hold paper, parchment or a book with a pen or pencil in your other hand and use **Record a Find** (Object tab) within two tiles of something wild. After about twenty seconds your record is written down.
  - Living animals count for Agriculture, wild plants for Medicine, ores and rocks for Natural Science, and the night sky, seen outdoors at night, for Basic Mathematics. The sky can be recorded once each season.
  - Weather and water count for Natural Science too. Stand outdoors in rain, a monsoon, snow, a blizzard, a sandstorm, a gale or a heat wave to record it, once per kind each season. Stand within two tiles of the sea, fresh water, a jungle river, a swamp, floodwater or frozen water to record that water.
  - In a book, the record adds a twentieth of a chapter to a subject of that discipline whose groundwork you know. If more than one fits, you choose.
  - On a sheet it becomes naturalist's notes. Use them on a bench researching any subject of that discipline.
  - A book takes each kind of find once, and finds can fill three tenths of a chapter at most, so look for different animals, plants and stones.
  - A **preserved specimen** on an **anatomy table** counts for Medicine. To make one, hold a whole raw organ in one hand and an open preserve jar holding at least 10 units of spirits or vinegar in the other, then use the organ on the anatomy table. The specimen can't be eaten or transplanted afterwards. Each specimen can be recorded three times. Every kind of organ is a different find, and an animal's heart is the same find as a human heart.
- **Compare notes.** When two books on the same bench each hold part of the same chapter, two scholars who could both research that subject can stand beside the bench and use **Compare Notes** (right-click the bench). The fuller book gains half of what the other one holds, and the other book keeps its own work. You need light, and both of you must be able to read both books. Each book can be compared once per subject. Both scholars learn from it.

Later subjects have their own [field work](#field-work).

**Hands-on work teaches you too.** Each piece of work written into a book, whether a deed, foraging, butchering, a prototype, a field study, a recorded find, a sample taken apart at the bench or a demonstration, also raises your own [scholarship](#scholarship) in the subject's discipline. It teaches half again as much as the same progress earned by waiting at the bench, and the skill goes to the person who did the work. Each piece of work teaches you once, so repeating it into a second book adds to the book but not to your skill.

### Losing an age

Each land keeps an age only as long as its capstone is written down somewhere in that land. Where an age has two capstones, either one keeps it. If every book, loose chapter page and recipe manual in the land that holds the current age's capstone is destroyed or carried to another land, and no capstone of a later age is left there either, the land keeps its age for two hours. If no copy comes back in that time, the land falls back one age and everyone there is told. If the age before it has lost its capstone too, that one goes an hour later. The Stone Age is never lost.

Nobody forgets anything when an age is lost. Anyone who still knows the lost capstone can **write it from memory** into a book, and the land returns to that age at once.

A capstone carried into a land that has not reached its age yet moves that land forward one age once it has stayed two hours, then one more each hour until the land reaches the book's age.

### How knowledge spreads

Old knowledge does not stay locked up. Every subject is in one of three states, set by how many ages its own age is behind the world's age. The tech tree and the book and bench windows show the state after the subject's age.

| State | Which subjects | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Guarded** | The world's age and the one before it | Nothing special. You research it, read it or learn it from someone, as described in this guide. |
| **Spreading** | Two and three ages behind | Its chapter takes **half the time** to read or to hear read aloud. [Watching and guided work](#teaching-and-apprentices) can teach you the **whole** subject, even a late one. Researching it at a bench goes **three times as fast**. You still need a book, a teacher or a bench. |
| **Common knowledge** | Four or more ages behind | **Everyone knows it.** A new character starts with it, and everyone else gains it when the world reaches the new age. |

For example, when the world reaches the Industrial Age, every Stone Age subject becomes common knowledge and the Classical and Medieval subjects are spreading. In the Digital Age everything up to WW1 is common, WW2 and the Cold War are spreading, and only the Modern and Digital subjects are guarded.

Common knowledge gives you the subjects only. It does not raise your scholarship or your crafting skills, and you still need the materials, tools and machines. You can write a common subject into a book from memory like anything else you know.

If the world [loses an age](#losing-an-age), nobody forgets what they gained. Characters made afterwards start with less, until the age is won back.

### Learning from things

You slowly come to understand a subject you do not know just by living with its things. It does not matter who made them.

- **Using a thing** from a subject you don't know teaches a little: holding it and working it, firing it, wearing it, or working a structure or machine by hand. The first time with each kind of thing teaches about **5%**, and every later use about **1%**, at most once every couple of minutes.
- **Being around things** teaches a little too: now and then you notice a new kind of thing near you, about **2%** for each kind.
- It is slow: at most **10% of one subject a day**.
- It **never teaches a whole subject on its own**. It can give you up to half of a Stone Age subject, and less of each later age, down to a sliver of the newest ones. The last step always needs a book, a teacher or a bench.

Everything you pick up this way counts toward the same progress as reading the chapter, [watching or working beside someone](#teaching-and-apprentices) or researching it, so it makes those quicker.

### The bench window

- **Books on the bench** shows both books and their chapters. A chapter you can study is marked, and clicking any chapter opens its details.
- **You have** lists what you are holding (writing tool and blank sheets) and your scholarship.
- **Currently researching** shows the progress bar, a checklist of what the work still needs (a book, a researcher near the bench, light, an instrument), a warning when supplies have run out, and roughly how long is left at the current pace. **Speed right now** lists everything that sets the pace: the scholar's skill, the writing tool, supplies, related chapters shelved nearby and any helper. Supplies keep the bench at full speed and never add a bonus; without them it runs at half speed. One scholar standing between two benches drives both at once. **Stop researching** halts it; the unfinished work stays in the book.
- **Find technology** searches by subject, recipe or discipline. The buttons under it show only what is **Ready to study**, what you **Can research**, what is **In progress**, what is **Known** or what is **Not yet** open to you. Switch between **Tree** and **List** to see the subjects as rows or as the tree.
- Click a subject for its details: the groundwork it needs (click one to jump to it), what researching it needs and whether you meet each point, how long studying, researching and writing it take, what it leads to and the recipes it unlocks.
- The buttons in the details do the work in one click: **Study this chapter**, **Research this here**, **Add to queue**, **Copy into the other book** and **Write it from memory**. In the list view each row has its own **Study** or **Write** button.
- **Queue** up to 12 subjects. When one is finished the bench starts the next one that is ready.

## Late research

From era 5 on (the ages after the Industrial Age), research is more than waiting at a bench. Passive bench work can fill the progress bar, but some chapters still need a prototype, a demonstration, a bill of materials or a combination of these. If the bar is full, check the subject details rather than waiting longer.

### Field work

Most late subjects list one or two pieces of **field work** in their details. Do the work anywhere, then stand at a bench that is researching the subject: your experience is written into the book as a share of the subject, usually between a fifth and a half. Each book takes each piece of field work once, and the bench window shows how far along you are.

| Field work | Counts towards |
| --- | --- |
| Firing firearms | Cartridge Firearms, Self-Loading Firearms, Automatic Weapons, Mechanized Warfare, Missiles, Modern Armor, Infantry Support Weapons, Guided Missiles |
| Flying an aircraft as its pilot | Aviation & Rocketry, Naval Aviation, Missiles, Rocketry, Crewed Flight, Pressure Suits, Drones |
| Completing surgery steps | Anesthetic Surgery, Modern Medicine, Emergency Medicine |
| Diagnosing sick patients with a doctor's handbook | Pharmacology, Germ Theory, Public Health, Emergency Medicine |
| Running syntheses at a reaction bench | Pharmacology, Synthetic Fabrics, Modern Medicine, Life Support, Local Resources, Combustion Engine |
| Smelting ore or metal | Combustion Engine, Alloys |
| Striking ore veins while mining | Alloys |
| Analysing survey samples at a mission console | Planetary Survey and the space subjects after it |
| Harvesting crops | Soil Science, Crop Breeding |

Your experience stays with your character, but it does not come with you when you travel to another region.

### Demonstrations

The ages from Steam Power on don't advance when you hand a part to the bench. Their chapters need a **demonstration**: the machine running in the world, watched by someone who writes down what they see. Bench study is still needed as well.

| Subject | Demonstration |
| --- | --- |
| Steam Power | A steam engine kept running for a minute |
| Electricity | An electric lamp lit from an engine or generator for a minute |
| Nuclear Power | A reactor producing power, below overheating, for three minutes |
| Digital Computing | A computerized fabricator kept running for a minute |
| Digital Networks | A mobile phone call kept connected through a cell tower for a minute |

1. Hold a sheet of paper or parchment, or a book, with a pen or pencil in your other hand. You need light, and you must know everything the subject builds on.
2. Use **Record a Demonstration** (Object tab) while the machine is running within three tiles. Stay close and keep it running until your notes are done.
3. Notes on paper become **demonstration notes**. Use them on a bench that is researching the subject, with its book on the bench. A book you recorded in holds the demonstration already.

The machine usually needs the very chapter it proves. If you carry a book holding at least half of the chapter, you can build a crude **experimental** version of the machine from the usual recipe. Experimental machines can fail during a demonstration (a steam engine can burst and scald anyone next to it) and fall apart after about twenty minutes.

### Bills of materials

Every capstone from Steam Power on, and the biggest late subjects, also need a **bill of materials**: a large stock of materials delivered to the bench while the subject's book is on it. The chapter is finished only once its study, its demonstration or prototype and its bill are all in the book.

| Subject | Bill |
| --- | --- |
| Steam Power | 60 coal, 40 iron ingots, 10 copper ingots |
| Electricity | 80 copper ingots, 30 zinc ingots, 20 lead ingots, 20 rubber sheets |
| Powered Flight | 120 steel sheets, 40 rubber sheets, 30 brass ingots, 40 cloth (in place of a prototype) |
| Mechanized Warfare | 200 steel sheets, 40 rubber sheets, 40 lead ingots, 30 sulphur, 30 saltpeter (in place of a prototype) |
| Automatic Weapons | 60 steel sheets, 30 lead ingots, 20 brass ingots |
| Aviation & Rocketry | 80 steel sheets, 30 aluminium ingots, 20 rubber sheets |
| Missiles | 60 steel sheets, 40 sulphur, 40 saltpeter |
| Nuclear Power | 30 uranium ore, 120 lead ingots, 150 steel sheets, 100 concrete |
| Composite Armour | 100 steel sheets, 60 aluminium ingots, 20 rubber sheets |
| Digital Computing | 250 copper ingots, 100 aluminium ingots, 60 gold ingots, 60 silver ingots, 60 electronics, 15 gems |
| Rocketry | 100 aluminium ingots, 80 steel sheets, 40 sulphur, 40 copper ingots |
| Orbital Flight | 200 aluminium ingots, 40 gold ingots, 30 silver ingots, 60 electronics |
| Digital Networks | 350 copper ingots, 150 aluminium ingots, 80 gold ingots, 80 silver ingots, 100 electronics, 25 gems |

- Use a stack of a listed material on the bench. The bench takes only what is still owed, and the research window lists what is left.
- Anyone can deliver, whatever their faction and whether or not they know the subject. Buying, trading and mining abroad all count the same.
- Deliveries are written into the book and travel with it. A copied book starts with nothing delivered, and a destroyed book loses what was in it.
- Some of these materials come mostly from particular lands, such as rubber from the jungle lands. See [Regions](../regions/Regions.md) for what each land is rich in.

### Knowing a subject

Some late subjects do more than unlock recipes. Once you have **studied** the chapter, you carry its benefit yourself; the details of each subject show it under **Knowing it**.

| Subject | What knowing it does |
| --- | --- |
| Alloys | Metal tools and weapons you forge last 30% longer. |
| Advanced Agriculture | Your harvests give 25% more crop and seed. |
| Anesthetic Surgery | Each surgery step you perform is 15% more likely to succeed. |
| Modern Medicine | You finish each surgery step a quarter faster. |
| Public Health | You are half as likely to catch an illness from people nearby or from poor hygiene. |
| Aviation & Rocketry | Aircraft you pilot burn a quarter less fuel in the air. |
| Soil Science | Your harvests leave the soil richer, so the same plot can be sown again sooner. |
| Crop Breeding | Your harvests give a further 25% more crop and seed. |

### Big science

Five of the largest subjects are meant for a team. Each wants a **second scholar** of another discipline standing at the bench, and a **facility** within three tiles of it.

| Subject | Second scholar | Facility |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nuclear Power | Engineering | A reaction bench |
| Digital Computing | Engineering | A foundry workbench |
| Rocketry | Engineering | A foundry workbench |
| Orbital Flight | Natural Science | A launch rail |
| Modern Medicine | Natural Science | An operating table |

The second scholar needs half the level that leading a subject of that age in their discipline would take. A lone scholar can still finish: without the second scholar the work runs at a quarter speed, and without the facility at half speed. On every other subject, in every age, research goes faster with help: up to two more scholars who could research the subject themselves each add half of their own pace, so a team of three works at up to double speed. The helpers stand at the bench with a writing tool in hand, and they gain scholarship from the work at half the lead's rate. **Speed right now** on the bench shows what the helpers add.

### Rushing

**Rush the work** in the bench window (**Work carefully** turns it off again) makes a late subject go 50% faster, with a small chance of an accident each time the bench makes progress. An accident undoes a little of the work and hurts: radiation for Nuclear Power, a blast for science and military subjects, poisoning for medicine and farming, a crushed hand for engineering, or just a mistake to throw out. The further your skill is above what the subject needs, the rarer accidents are. Careful work is the default and never has accidents.

### Later subjects

Health, Agriculture and Military carry on past the subjects listed in the [node reference](Guide_to_Research.md#full-node-reference):

| Subject | Era | Needs | Gives |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Soil Science | 7 | Advanced Agriculture | Richer soil after your harvests |
| Infantry Support Weapons | 7 | Field Artillery, Automatic Weapons | Foldable mortar, mortar shells and smoke shells, flamethrower hose and fuel backpack, anti-personnel and anti-tank mines (all steel) |
| Emergency Medicine | 8 | Modern Medicine, Anesthetic Surgery | Defibrillator and power cells (electronics), autoinjectors and body bags (plastic) |
| Crop Breeding | 8 | Advanced Agriculture | Bigger harvests |
| Guided Missiles | 8 | Missiles, Infantry Support Weapons | Foldable anti-tank guided missile system and its HEAT and HE missiles (steel) |

A crafted flamethrower backpack is empty and takes up to 100 units of gasoline; wear it on your back and hold the hose with your other hand empty. Mines are crafted unarmed: use one in hand to deploy it, and disarm an armed mine with wirecutters. A mine detector (Electricity) finds armed mines nearby.

A crafted autoinjector is empty: fill it from a container like a syringe. It injects its whole dose in a second and works once.

## Writing and copying books

Books are ordinary items. They can be traded, lent, hidden, stolen or carried to another region, and they also still work as journals.

| Action | How | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Write notes** | Use a pencil or pen on a book and choose **Contents** to add a page of your own writing. Use the book in hand to read it. | Instant |
| **Write from memory** | Put the book down next to you, hold a pencil or pen and Alt-click the book, then press **Write** beside a chapter you know. At a bench, press **Write it from memory** in the subject's details. A blank sheet of paper, papyrus or parchment is taken from your other hand, your bags or a paper bundle you carry (taking one off a bundle needs a free hand). | 2 minutes in the Stone Age, +1 minute per later age; costs stamina |
| **Copy at a bench** | Put two books on a bench, open a chapter and press **Copy into the other book**, or press the **Copy all ... chapters** button under a book to copy everything the other book lacks, one after another. Hold a pencil or pen and have blank sheets on you. You don't need to know the chapter. | Same as writing |
| **Rip out a page** | Hold the book, click the chapter in its window and press **Tear this chapter out**. A chapter becomes a loose page that can be studied on its own. | 2 seconds |
| **Bind a page back in** | Use the loose page on a book. | 30 seconds |
| **Print a book** | Put the book in a printing press as the original and a blank book as the copy, then use the press. It prints the pages, title and every finished chapter, using one sheet of printing paper per chapter. Load printing paper by using blank paper, papyrus, parchment, a paper bundle or a paper bin on the press. Unfinished research isn't printed. | 25 seconds |

### Ciphers

A scholar who knows **Printing Press** can lock a research book. Use a pen on the book, choose **Cipher** and set a key word. It takes 30 seconds.

- Nobody can study from an enciphered book, copy its chapters or tear pages out of it without the key.
- Tell the key word to anyone you trust. They use a pen on the book, choose **Cipher** and enter it once; after that the book stays open to them. The same menu lets a reader remove the cipher.
- The cipher is as strong as its writer's Basic Mathematics: strength 1 with no training, one more for every 20 levels, up to 6.
- A scholar with at least 12 levels of Basic Mathematics and a pen can **break** a cipher without the key, in 30 second sittings. A strength 1 cipher takes about two minutes for an equal; stronger ciphers and better writers take longer, up to three times as long, and a much better code-breaker takes as little as half. Progress is kept with the book, so you can come back to it.
- A printing press copies the cipher along with the book. An enciphered book still counts as a written record of its age.

Recipe manuals and recipe compendiums take written notes too: use the book and choose **Read the notes** or study as usual. Binding manuals together keeps their notes. Research chapters only go into ordinary books, and a manual with notes in it can't be carried to another region.

A bench holds up to two books. Research goes into the first one; **Write research into the other book** swaps them.

## Scholarship

Research trains six disciplines, from 0 to 100: **Basic Mathematics, Engineering, Natural Science, Medicine, Agriculture** and **Military Science**. The **Scholarship** tab in the side panel shows your level in each discipline, the latest age you can research in it, your title, your master and your apprentices; the bench shows what each chapter needs.

- Stone Age research needs no training. Each later age needs 12 more levels in the chapter's discipline, plus half that in Basic Mathematics. A Bronze Age chapter needs 12 and 6; an Imperial Age chapter needs 36 and 18.
- Skill above the requirement makes research faster, up to twice as fast.
- You gain skill by working at a bench, by studying chapters, by writing them and by [hands-on work](#learning-by-doing) that goes into a book. Work far below your level teaches you less, and half of what you learn also goes to Basic Mathematics.
- **Mastering a body skill teaches its discipline.** When one of your skills reaches 150 you become skilled and gain 4 levels; at 200 you become an expert and gain 8; at 250, the most a skill can reach, you are a master and gain 12. Medical teaches Medicine, crafting teaches Engineering, farming and hunting teach Agriculture, philosophy teaches Basic Mathematics, and swords, bows, rifles, pistols, machine guns and throwing teach Military Science. Each step pays a discipline once, so a second weapon skill does not pay Military Science again.

### Fields of study

If you want to focus, you can take an optional field of study: **Engineer, Strategist, Naturalist, Agronomist** or **Physician**. Take one in character setup, or later with **Choose field of study** (IC tab). It speeds research in its discipline (x1.1 for Engineering up to x1.4 for Medicine and Agriculture) and makes scholarship there grow 25% faster. In exchange, the other disciplines research at x0.9, grow 25% slower and take 15% longer to study. Basic Mathematics is never affected. Your field shows in the Scholarship tab. You can drop it at any time, and after any change you must wait 7 real days before picking a new one. The Autistic and ADHD traits also change how you study. See [Fields of study](Guide_to_Character.md#fields-of-study) and [Study traits](Guide_to_Character.md#study-traits).

## Practice

You can also train a discipline by hand. Hold its practice tool and use it in hand once: you keep practising, a little at a time, until you use it again, move, put it down, use **Stop Exercise**, run out of stamina or lose the light. Practice slowly uses up your stamina, which does not recover while you practise, so a rested character can keep at it for about four minutes. It trains only that discipline. A writing desk beside you makes you learn faster, and your master standing nearby doubles what you learn.

| Discipline | Practice tool | How to make it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic Mathematics | Writing slate, or an **abacus** for 50% more | 2 stone (no knowledge needed); abacus 3 soft wood (Trade & Coinage) |
| Engineering | **Model kit**, or a drafting set for 50% more | 3 soft wood (Basic Toolmaking); drafting set 2 iron (Iron Tools) |
| Natural Science | **Specimen box** | 2 soft wood (Basic Toolmaking) |
| Medicine | **Study bones** | 2 bone (Basic Toolmaking) |
| Agriculture | **Seed tray** | 2 soft wood (Basic Toolmaking) |
| Military Science | **War game board** | 2 soft wood (Basic Toolmaking) |

Practice is a way to get started, not a way to the top. Practice teaches a little, less as you improve, and practice stops at level 24, enough to research two ages past a discipline's first and to earn the Apprentice title. Beyond that, only research, hands-on work, study and tutoring teach you more.

## Teaching and apprentices

**Tutoring:** stand beside someone while they study a chapter you know. They finish in two thirds of the time, and if you're still there at the end they gain three times the usual skill and you gain some too. Selling books or tutoring is ordinary trade, so set your own price.

**Reading aloud:** hold a book, open it and press **Read aloud** on a chapter you already know (or right-click the book and choose **Read Chapter Aloud**). Everyone within hearing who has the chapter's groundwork takes it in at half the pace of reading it themselves, and they can keep working while they listen. Walls don't matter, only distance. What they hear counts toward the same chapter in any book, so a listener can finish it by reading later. You need light to read by, you must stay put, and you stop by moving. If you are presenting at a broadcast station, people beside a set tuned to it hear the reading too. You gain a little skill each time a listener finishes.

**Learning a trade by hand:** you can learn a subject from someone who knows it without opening a book, as long as you already know what the subject builds on.

- **Watch.** Stand within 2 tiles while they make one of the subject's things. Each different thing you watch fills 15% of your study of the subject, up to three different things.
- **Work under their eye.** While they stay within 2 tiles and awake, you can make the subject's things yourself in the crafting menu, at an anvil or at a workbench. Each one fills another 20%. Some attempts are spoiled: about 3 in 10 at ordinary crafting skill, far fewer for skilled hands. A spoiled attempt uses up the materials but still fills 10%.
- This is the same progress that reading fills, so you can mix the two, and whatever you have learned by hand shortens the reading.
- Subjects from before WW1 can be learned wholly this way. From WW1 on, hands can show you half, and the rest has to be read from the chapter.
- The teacher gains scholarship for each pupil they bring through a subject.

Books are still the only way to send knowledge to another land or to keep it after its last master dies.

**Apprentices:** use **Take an apprentice** in the IC tab on a player next to you. They have to accept, and a master can have up to three. While their master is within 2 tiles, an apprentice gains twice the usual skill at a bench, and apprentices watching their master research within 2 tiles pick up a little skill too. The apprentice can end it with **Leave apprenticeship**, and it ends when the apprentice dies.

**Titles:** examining someone shows their best discipline as a title once it reaches 12: **Apprentice** from 12, **Scholar** from 40 and **Master** from 75, for example "Scholar of Medicine". Use it to find someone to hire.

## Tips

- Copy your important chapters. A raid on your library takes every chapter you only had once.
- Keep spare copies of each capstone in different places. If the last one in a land burns or leaves, that land loses the age until a copy comes back or someone who knows it writes it down again.
- A scribe doesn't need to be a scholar: anyone can copy chapters at a bench.
- Got a newcomer and no spare books? Have them stand by while you work, then let them try. A few pieces later they know the trade.
- New to a discipline? Practise with its tool between study sessions, then rest while your stamina recovers.
- Short on scholarship for the next age? Do the subject's deeds, prototypes and field studies yourself instead of waiting at the bench. They teach more.
- Keep a bench lit, stocked and next to your bookcases, and research with a pen at a desk nearby.
- Rip out single chapters to lend or sell, and keep the master book at home.
